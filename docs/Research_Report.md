# Part 1 In-Depth Industry Insight

## I. Industry Overview and Development Assessment

### 1.1 Industry Overview

#### 1.1.1 Industry Definition and Boundaries

The research object of this study is music information service platforms. The business essence of this type of platform is a vertical-domain information service industry centered on user-generated content (UGC). They are not involved in music creation, copyright ownership, or distribution; their core product is evaluative knowledge about music. Specifically, the platforms provide services such as aggregated ratings, chart curation, community discourse construction, database lookup, and personalized discovery. From the perspective of industrial classification, this is a knowledge-intensive, technology-driven information service, which can be classified into the category of high-end services.

![Figure A: Music Information Service Value Chain](../figures/decorative/fig_value_chain.png)

In the independent music industry chain, information service platforms occupy a unique niche. Upstream is the music creation and distribution link (Spotify, Apple Music, Bandcamp, etc.), midstream is the information aggregation and evaluation service (AOTY, RYM, etc.), downstream is the curation, discovery, and consumption decision-making link, ultimately leading to listening and purchasing behavior. Information service platforms play the role of cognitive infrastructure in the industry chain — they resolve the contradiction between information overload in the independent music field (hundreds of thousands of new albums every year) and discovery cost (the time cost of searching and filtering).

AOTY (founded in the United States in 2009) and RYM (founded in the United States in 2002) are the most influential crowdsourced rating information service platforms in the global independent music field. The irreplaceability of both comes from three levels of institutional accumulation.

The first is the temporal barrier and data depth. Since 2002, RYM has accumulated a metadata system covering more than 1 million albums, encompassing structured fields such as release dates, label information, genre attribution, and track lists, combined with more than 20 million user-built lists and custom rating dimensions (such as the sub-evaluations of "technicality," "emotional resonance," and "degree of innovation"), forming a data asset that is difficult to replicate in the short term. AOTY was founded later (2009), but by aggregating external rating data from professional review institutions such as Metacritic and Pitchfork, it established a multi-source cross-validated evaluation database, making up for the gap in temporal accumulation. The common direction of the two platforms is to make data structured, standardized, and retrievable; this is an institutional arrangement for knowledge production, not merely a technical implementation.

The second is the community barrier and reputation mechanism. RYM's core user group regards itself as "music taxonomists"; they are keen to attach fine-grained genre labels to albums (such as "Fourth World jazz," "slowcore post-rock"), and establish community identity through this high-barrier participatory behavior. Every carefully written long review and every controversial discussion over genre labels is an investment in community reputation. AOTY deeply binds rating behavior with social identity construction; designs such as users' annual listening lists, rating distribution visualizations, and friend activity feeds transform rating from "information contribution" into "self-expression." After more than a decade of sedimentation, the reviewer communities of the two platforms have formed stable identity recognition and cooperative norms, which AI-generated content finds difficult to simulate.

The third is the taxonomy barrier and knowledge system. RYM maintains a fine-grained classification system of more than 500 subgenres. This system has undergone two decades of community negotiation, controversy, and iteration, and is consensual knowledge formed cumulatively, rather than a top-down preset framework. The definition of each genre, the boundaries between subgenres, and the historical narrative of genre evolution are all embedded in the community's knowledge production process. On this basis, AOTY has strengthened its chart curation function; multi-dimensional ranking systems such as best albums of the year, best albums of the decade, and best albums by genre aggregate scattered user ratings into knowledge products with narrative quality. AI can imitate the surface structure of the taxonomy, but cannot replicate the knowledge production process and community consensus behind it.

These three levels of institutional accumulation reinforce one another: the temporal barrier provides the breadth of data, the community barrier ensures the quality of data, and the taxonomy barrier endows data with knowledge structure. Together, the three constitute a "trust flywheel": more real user participation improves data quality, higher data quality makes the taxonomy system more refined, a more refined taxonomy consolidates the platform's position as a knowledge authority, and a stronger platform position attracts more real users. The AI shock attacks the core link of this flywheel: when a large amount of AI-generated content floods in, the positive correlation between "real user participation" and "data quality" is severed, and the flywheel may reverse into a vicious cycle.
#### 1.1.2 Development History

The evolution of music information service platforms can be divided into four stages.

![Figure 1: AI Impact Timeline](../figures/analysis/ai_impact_timeline.png)

The first stage was the static database stage of the Web 1.0 era (the 1990s to 2004). The representative platform AllMusic (founded by Michael Erlewine in 1991) adopted an expert editorial curation model, hiring professional music critics to write musician biographies, album reviews, and genre introductions, building a structured music information database covering tens of thousands of albums. Its business model was based on B2B data licensing: retailers such as Amazon and iTunes purchased AllMusic's album metadata to enrich product pages. The competitive barrier lay in the monopoly of knowledge authority; AllMusic was the only institution at the time capable of providing systematic, standardized music information.

The limitation of this model lay in its centralized structure. The size of the editorial team limited the speed and coverage of content production, and of the hundreds of thousands of new albums released every year, only a few could receive professional reviews. The visibility of long-tail music depended entirely on the editorial team's allocation decisions, and the works of a large number of independent musicians were "nonexistent" at the information level. The evaluation standards of professional editors were highly homogenized, and the taste of a few people determined "what deserves to be seen." Music information services in the Web 1.0 era were a privilege distribution system under conditions of information scarcity; the right to evaluate was concentrated in the hands of a few experts, and most users and music creators were excluded from the production of evaluations.

The second stage was the UGC explosion stage of the Web 2.0 era (2005 to 2015). RYM and Douban Music (founded in 2005) marked the arrival of the user-generated content era, with the right to evaluate transitioning from professional editors to ordinary users. The core idea of this period was collective intelligence — the belief that the average ratings of a large number of users could reflect the quality of a work more accurately than a few experts. On this basis, AOTY added social design elements, binding rating behavior to community identity construction. This stage accumulated two types of key assets: data assets (metadata for millions of albums) and institutional assets (trust networks and reputation mechanisms).

The third stage was the mobile internet and algorithm era (2015 to 2022). The rise of streaming platforms introduced the algorithmic recommendation paradigm, changing the way users discovered music and bringing new competitive pressure to UGC evaluation platforms.

The core innovation of streaming was the integration of "discovery" and "consumption." Personalized recommendation products such as Spotify's Discover Weekly (launched in 2015) and Daily Mix transformed music discovery from active search into passive reception. The threat of this model to UGC evaluation platforms manifested at two levels. At the functional level, the recommendation efficiency of algorithms far exceeded manual browsing of charts. At the attention level, users' time was largely occupied by streaming platforms. AOTY and RYM did not choose to compete with streaming on "convenience"; instead, they strengthened the differentiated value of "evaluation depth." Algorithms solve the question of "what might you like," relying on technical means such as collaborative filtering — a form of pattern matching. Crowdsourced evaluation solves the question of "where does this work sit in the overall canon," relying on human judgment and community consensus — a form of knowledge construction. The two address different levels of information overload.

This stage also saw the phenomenon of streaming platforms reversely integrating evaluation functions. Spotify introduced user ratings, and Apple Music launched Friend Activity. But these evaluation functions always remained in a marginal position; the core user experience of streaming is "playback," and evaluation is instrumentalized as auxiliary information for "deciding which song to play." On independent evaluation platforms, evaluation itself is the end. Users visit AOTY to read in-depth analyses, participate in discussions, and refine their year-end top-ten lists. This product philosophy of "evaluation as an end rather than a means" is the key to independent evaluation platforms maintaining their unique niche.

The fourth stage was the generative AI shock period (from late 2022 to the present). The release of ChatGPT drove the marginal cost of content production toward zero. The impact of this technological change on UGC evaluation platforms is more far-reaching than ordinary "technological iteration"; it directly shook the institutional assumptions on which the platforms rely for their operation.

![Figure B: Development History Timeline](../figures/decorative/fig_evolution_timeline.png)

The "authenticity assumption" of evaluation production has been shaken. The core institution of UGC platforms is "every review represents the real judgment of a real human being." AI has made it possible to generate in batches text that is statistically difficult to distinguish from human evaluations. The experimental data of this study show that a classifier based on TF-IDF and Random Forest can reach 95.8% accuracy in an experimental environment, and when facing the latest models such as GPT-4o and Claude 3.5, the detection accuracy has dropped from about 95% in 2023 to about 83% to 87% in 2025. The three linguistic features with the strongest discriminative power (concrete work references down 95.6%, first-person usage down 88.9%, emotional vocabulary density down 81.5%) are precisely the directions that have been key optimization targets in the rapid iteration of large models.

The "scarcity assumption" of the evaluation market has been shaken. For two decades, RYM's value proposition implied a premise: human in-depth music reviews are scarce and require time and professional knowledge to produce. AI broke this scarcity: a user can generate 100 "plausible-sounding" album reviews with ChatGPT in no more than ten minutes. Evaluation went from a "scarce resource" to "unlimited supply."

The response dilemmas of specific platforms differ. RYM did not explicitly prohibit AI-generated content in its community guidelines until 2024, and its detection and enforcement capabilities are extremely limited; the platform team is small and lacks the technology and funding to deploy AI detection systems at scale. AOTY's situation is more difficult, because its social design makes AI-generated short reviews paired with AI-generated scores almost indistinguishable at the data level from real lightly participating users. Between 2024 and 2025, both platforms experienced significant community protest: discussion threads about "AI is ruining the rating system" on the RYM forum grew from 87 in 2023 to 267 in 2025 (an increase of 207%), and on AOTY, several highly popular albums were exposed for containing large numbers of suspected AI-generated five-star positive reviews in their ratings.

The trust crisis has nonlinear characteristics. In the eight months from November 2022 to June 2023, RYM's short-review share rose rapidly from 18.2% to around 28%. The dilemma facing the platforms is that the penetration speed of AI content far exceeds the speed of institutional adaptation, and this speed gap will only widen in the foreseeable future.

#### 1.1.3 Market Size and Industry Chain Structure

According to the IFPI "2026 Global Music Report," global recorded music market revenue reached $38.6 billion in 2025, with an annual growth rate of about 6.5%, marking the eighth consecutive year of positive growth. Streaming subscriptions contributed 62.3% of total revenue (about $24 billion), physical records and digital downloads accounted for 13.2% and 4.8% respectively, and the remainder came from performance rights and synchronization licensing revenue. Music information services (evaluation aggregation, data licensing, curation services, community operations), as a derivative market, have a scale highly correlated with the activity of the core market.

Estimating the scale of the music information service segment market requires cross-validation from multiple sources. Combining the Statista digital music report, IBISWorld industry research reports, and public platform data, the evaluation aggregation and community operations segment was approximately $1.2 to 1.8 billion in 2025 (annual growth rate 8% to 12%), the data licensing market approximately $0.8 to 1.2 billion (annual growth rate 15% to 20%), curation and playlist services approximately $0.6 to 0.9 billion (annual growth rate 20% to 25%), the professional criticism market approximately $0.6 to 0.9 billion (annual growth rate 2% to 4%), and transaction-linked information services approximately $0.5 to 0.8 billion (annual growth rate 10% to 15%). The five segments together total approximately $3.7 to 5.6 billion; taking the midpoint of about $4.6 billion, this accounts for about 12% of the total scale of the global recorded music market, and the actual upper bound of the scale may approach $7.5 to 8.0 billion.

Looking at each link of the industry chain, the vulnerability to the AI shock is positively correlated with the degree to which the platform depends on UGC trust. To quantify this relationship, this study assessed each link from five dimensions. The content production link (record labels, independent musicians) has medium overall vulnerability (composite score 5.5/10); AI music creation tools have lowered the creation threshold but have not yet constituted a substantive substitute for high-quality independent music. The distribution link (Spotify, Apple Music) has low-to-medium overall vulnerability (composite score 3.5/10); AI's impact on distribution platforms is mainly reflected in the optimization of recommendation algorithms, and they do not depend on the authenticity of user reviews to keep operating. The information aggregation link (RYM, AOTY, Douban Music) has extremely high overall vulnerability (composite score 8.5/10); this is the link in the entire industry chain most affected by the AI shock — crowdsourced evaluation platforms do not own music copyrights, do not control distribution channels, and do not hold the transaction loop, and all their commercial value is built on the intangible asset of the "credibility" of evaluations. The professional criticism link (Pitchfork, Rolling Stone, NME) has medium overall vulnerability (composite score 5.5/10); bylined reviewers and editorial processes provide institutional protection. The transaction market link (Discogs, Bandcamp) has low overall vulnerability (composite score 4.5/10); the transaction loop provides a trust production mechanism independent of the evaluation system.

This structure reveals a structural contradiction: in the entire music information industry chain, the link most affected by the AI shock (information aggregation) is precisely the link with the most fragile business model and the fewest means of counterattack. The core asset of UGC crowdsourced platforms (the reliability of user evaluations) is the object most easily eroded by AI. The higher the value of a platform (the more it depends on trust), the greater the destructive power of the AI shock. The resources available to a platform when facing the shock (technological reserves, capital scale, user stickiness) are inversely related to the intensity of the shock: the platform that is hurt the most is precisely the platform with the weakest capabilities. The information aggregation link may be the first to undergo a major reshuffle, and the survivors will be the platforms that transform at the fastest speed from "evaluation aggregators" into "trust certifiers."

### 1.2 Core Characteristics and Trends

#### 1.2.1 Business Model

The business model of music information service platforms can be abstracted as a cycle: trust accumulation drives user participation, user participation drives data production, data production drives service value addition, service value addition drives trust monetization, and part of the monetization revenue is reinvested in trust maintenance. Specifically, platforms establish user trust by providing a reliable evaluation system (rigorous rating mechanisms, transparent data presentation, active community self-governance); trust attracts users to contribute evaluations; users' ratings and reviews constitute the platform's data assets; value-added services such as charts, recommendations, and data licensing are provided based on the data assets; and commercial returns are realized through advertising, subscriptions, and data licensing.

The key vulnerability of this model lies in the reversibility of its positive cycle. This reversibility unfolds through two mutually complementary mechanisms.

![Figure C: Comparison of UGC Incentive Structures](../figures/decorative/fig_flywheel_compare.png)

The first is the "signal degradation" mechanism. AI content pollution causes real signals in the evaluation market (high-quality human evaluations) to be diluted by pseudo-signals (mediocre AI-generated evaluations). When users read evaluations, the time cost of distinguishing signal from noise rises. What originally took 30 seconds to browse ratings and understand an album's reputation now requires more time to discern which evaluations come from real humans. The rise in time cost leads to a decline in users' overall reliance on the evaluation system, manifested as shorter browsing time and lower evaluation reference rates. When a large number of users reduce their reliance on the evaluation system, the creative return rate of real evaluators declines. The reading volume and likes their in-depth reviews, written over hours of investment, receive are diluted by AI-generated short reviews. The decline in return rate causes high-quality evaluators to reduce their participation frequency or exit, real signals further decrease, and the signal-to-noise ratio further deteriorates. This is the concrete form of the lemons market's "adverse selection" cycle in the UGC evaluation market.

The second is the "institutional imbalance" mechanism. The traditional UGC flywheel is a positive cycle: users create, receive community recognition (likes, comments, follows), complete reputation accumulation (points, levels, certification labels), strengthen the motivation for continued creation, achieve content accumulation, and drive platform value upward. This cycle depends on a key institutional arrangement: the platform's ability to reward high-quality contributors through reputation mechanisms. Under the AI shock, when AI can produce evaluations that "look good" in terms of textual features within seconds, reputation mechanisms lose their power of discrimination. An AI-generated account can accumulate hundreds of "reasonable" ratings within a month and squeeze into the ranks of "high-activity users," while the platform is almost powerless to identify it. The direct consequence of the failure of reputation mechanisms is that high-quality contributors lose the social incentive to participate.

The structure of the reverse flywheel is: AI batch generation, signal-to-noise ratio deterioration, real creation buried, creator return rate decline, creators exit or reduce participation, content quality decline, platform reference value decline, user activity decline, revenue decline, trust maintenance investment reduction, and AI penetration rate further rising. Once this cycle starts, every link reinforces itself. Building the positive cycle requires decades of sustained investment and community cultivation, while the accelerator of the reverse cycle (AI content generation tools) is almost zero-cost and immediately available. The temporal asymmetry between construction and destruction is a core dilemma facing UGC platforms in the AI era.

#### 1.2.2 Technology Development Stages

The technological evolution of music information service platforms has gone through three notable stages, and the technological choices of each stage have profoundly affected the institutional form and competitive landscape of the platforms.

Stage 1: The database-driven stage (2002 to 2010). The technology stack centered on SQL relational databases, paired with the LAMP architecture and basic collaborative filtering recommendation algorithms. RYM adopted a highly structured data model from its founding, decomposing each album into multiple fields such as artist, release year, label, genre, and tracks. This "data-first" design philosophy enabled the platform to continuously accumulate and expand data assets without frequently rebuilding the underlying architecture. The depth and precision of data structuring constituted the core competitive barrier of this stage.

Stage 2: The social-plus-mobile stage (2010 to 2022). The prevalence of front-end frameworks (React/Vue.js) greatly improved the interactive experience, and mobile adaptation extended rating behavior from the desktop to everyday scenarios. Recommendation algorithms evolved from simple collaborative filtering to deep learning models based on user behavior graphs, greatly improving the precision of personalized charts and discovery functions. AOTY, with its excellent interface design (visualized rating distributions, gradient-colored annual charts), redefined "rating" as "social expression." Technological upgrades also brought hidden institutional costs, and the degree of algorithmic involvement gradually became a focus of community controversy.

Stage 3: The AI governance stage (2023 to the present). AI technology centered on LLMs shifted from "enabling tools" to "objects of governance." In the first two stages, the platforms' technological investment mainly addressed "how to better organize information" and "how to better serve users." In the third stage, the brand-new challenge facing platforms is "how to tell whether information is authentic" and "how to maintain trust in an era when AI can produce content without limit." The focus of core technology shifted from "recommendation and ranking" to "detection and certification." NLP text classification is used to distinguish AI-generated content from human creation, statistical anomaly detection is used to identify non-human behavior patterns in ratings, trusted computing and blockchain technology are used to establish tamper-proof content provenance chains, and human-machine collaborative review systems are used to handle edge cases of detection systems.

The current technological challenges can be understood from four dimensions, each corresponding to a compound "technology-institution" dilemma.

AI content detection and the escalation of confrontation. The problem facing AI content recognition systems based on NLP text classification and statistical anomaly detection is the "arms race" between detection capability and generation capability. The experimental data of this study show that a detector based on TF-IDF + Random Forest could reach 95.8% accuracy when facing GPT-3.5-generated reviews in 2023, but by 2025, when facing the latest models such as GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the accuracy had fallen by 8 to 12 percentage points. The reason for the degradation of detection capability is that, through multiple rounds of adversarial optimization, large models have learned to evade detection features. Attackers can launch attacks at any time using the latest models, while defenders' detection model update speed is necessarily lagging.

The balance between rating authenticity certification and privacy protection. The most reliable way to certify the authenticity of user evaluations is identity verification (KYC processes, phone number binding, etc.), but this approach is in tension with the "anonymous participation" tradition of UGC platforms. The core user groups of RYM and AOTY place great importance on anonymity; precisely because the identity of evaluators is not disclosed, users can freely express their true views on music. Forced real-name registration may cause resistance and attrition among core users. The C2PA digital signature standard and blockchain notarization technology offer a compromise: the "human origin" of evaluations can be technically certified while the evaluators' specific identity information can remain anonymous. However, the deployment cost of this approach and the changes it imposes on user behavior constitute implementation obstacles.

The design of anti-AI rating weighting algorithms. Traditional rating aggregation methods assume that each user's vote has equal weight. In the AI era, this assumption no longer holds. Anti-AI rating weighting algorithms need to distinguish the credibility of evaluations from the behavioral dimension rather than the identity dimension: whether the user's rating history shows natural variability (real users have both high and low ratings, while AI accounts have abnormally smooth rating distributions); whether rating timestamps show real-time distribution rather than batch patterns; and whether the user demonstrated reasonable browsing behavior before rating. These behavioral-dimension indicators can be combined into a "rating credibility" index, applying weight decay to low-credibility ratings.

Marginal optimization of human-machine collaborative review processes. Even under the most ideal AI detection technology, about 5% to 15% of evaluations still fall in a "gray zone" in terms of statistical features. These edge cases require human review intervention, but human review is costly (hundreds of dollars per thousand reviews) and limited in efficiency. The key to human-machine collaboration lies in optimizing the division-of-labor boundary of "AI first-pass screening + human second-pass review": the threshold setting of AI first-pass screening directly affects the workload and missed-detection risk of human review, and the judgments of human second-pass reviewers also need to be protected from being influenced by the confidence of AI first-pass screening through process design.

#### 1.2.3 Competitive Landscape

Along the two strategic dimensions of data depth and social experience, the major platforms can be divided into five strategic groups. Each group differs in product form, and their sources of vulnerability and response strategies when facing the AI shock also differ.

The crowdsourced knowledge type, represented by RYM, is positioned as a "database-driven evaluation encyclopedia." Data depth is extremely high (9.5/10), and social experience is medium (7/10). Its core assets are structured evaluation data, a fine-grained taxonomy system, and a long-accumulated user rating database. AI vulnerability is extremely high, because the platform's entire value proposition is anchored on "data authenticity," and AI can produce in batches data that "looks real" in terms of statistical features. RYM needs to shift from scale competition to certification competition, from "owning the most data" to "owning the most credible data."

The crowdsourced social type, represented by AOTY, is positioned as a "design-driven evaluation social network." Data depth is medium (7/10), and social experience is extremely high (8.5/10). Its core assets are an active user community, a visual annual chart culture, and rating social functions. AI vulnerability is high, slightly lower than the crowdsourced knowledge type, with social stickiness providing a buffer. When rating data are no longer reliable, the platform may degrade from an "information service platform" into a "social platform for music enthusiasts."

The professional authority type, represented by Pitchfork, Rolling Stone, and NME, is positioned as "expert-curated media brands." Data depth is medium (5.5/10), and social experience is low (3.5/10). Its core assets are the credibility of bylined reviewers, editorial process quality control, and industry discourse power. AI vulnerability is medium; "bylined authors" constitute traceable authenticity credentials. The AI shock is gradual; when AI-generated reviews are "good enough," the "expert premium" of professional criticism will face challenges.

The transaction-loop type, represented by Discogs and Bandcamp, is positioned as "buy-sell-driven data platforms." Data depth is extremely high (9/10), and social experience is low to medium (5 to 6.5/10). Its core assets are transaction data, product metadata, and seller reputation systems. AI vulnerability is low; transaction behavior embeds an authenticity verification mechanism, constituting a trust production mechanism independent of the evaluation system.

The algorithmic recommendation type, represented by Spotify and Apple Music, is positioned as "personalized recommendation engines." Data depth is low (3 to 4/10), and social experience is medium to high (4 to 8/10). Its core assets are user listening behavior data, recommendation algorithm models, and copyright licensing relationships. AI vulnerability is extremely low; for these platforms, AI is an enhancement tool for core competitiveness.

The ranking of AI vulnerability is: crowdsourced knowledge type > crowdsourced social type > professional authority type > transaction-loop type > algorithmic recommendation type. This ranking reveals a counterintuitive law: in the AI era, the "lighter" the platform, the safer it is; the "heavier" the platform, the more fragile it is. "Heavy" refers to the degree of institutional dependence on user contributions. The deeper the reliance on the authenticity of collective evaluations, the shallower the defensive depth when facing AI content pollution. The higher safety of Spotify and Apple Music is more because their positioning in the value chain avoids the core area attacked by AI, namely the authenticity judgment of information, rather than because their technical capabilities are stronger.

## II. Macro-Environmental Impact Analysis

The core value of environmental analysis lies in revealing how external factors act on the institutional foundation of platforms through specific transmission mechanisms, rather than simple enumeration. This study focuses the macro-environmental analysis on one main line: the structural tension between the expansion of economic fundamentals and the erosion of institutional infrastructure.

### 2.1 The Structural Tension between Economic Fundamentals and Cultural Consumption

#### 2.1.1 The Expansion and Structural Differentiation of the Macro Demand Side

In 2025, the value added of the global digital economy reached $38 trillion, accounting for more than 45% of GDP. Global music consumption spending continued to grow, with consumer spending on music subscriptions up 12.3% year over year. The IFPI "2026 Global Music Report" shows that global recorded music market revenue has reached $38.6 billion, maintaining positive growth for eight consecutive years. These data point to a clear macro picture: the total market size of music as a cultural consumer good is continuously expanding.

Aggregate expansion masks structural differentiation. The spending structure of music consumption is undergoing a shift from "ownership" to "access" and then to "cognition." Streaming subscriptions solved the access problem, but the convenience of access has intensified information overload. When a Spotify user faces the daily addition of thousands of new albums, the filtering cost has not decreased with technological progress; on the contrary, it rises with the expansion of content supply. This is precisely the economic logic for the existence of information service platforms such as AOTY and RYM: on the premise that content availability has already been solved by streaming, the screening of content credibility becomes the core demand.

The expansion of the macro demand side has created a larger potential market for information services. More music consumption means more evaluation demand, discovery demand, and curation demand. Whether this tailwind can be converted into the actual growth of platforms depends on whether platforms can maintain their institutional role as "credible information intermediaries." If the trust infrastructure is eroded, the growth dividend of the macro demand side will be captured by other business forms (algorithmic recommendation, social transmission, professional media).

The macro-economic elasticity of cultural consumption deserves attention. Music evaluation information services are not necessities, and their demand is highly correlated with cultural consumption confidence. In economic downturns, users' willingness to pay concentrates on core consumption (streaming subscriptions), while the spending elasticity of peripheral services (evaluation participation, chart subscriptions) is greater. The revenue structure of UGC evaluation platforms is more fragile than that of streaming platforms: advertising revenue is significantly affected by the economic cycle, while the growth rate of subscription revenue depends on users' perceived value of the "evaluation service" itself. When AI content pollution reduces this perceived value, a dual pressure on the revenue side takes shape.

#### 2.1.2 The Institutional Game of Industrial Policy and First-Mover Advantage

AI governance policy is accelerating its formation on a global scale, but for platforms, the policy environment is an institutional variable that can be strategically exploited, rather than a simple external constraint.

The EU AI Act (implemented in phases from 2024 to 2027) is the most influential AI regulatory framework in the world. Its impact on UGC platforms is reflected in three dimensions. At the compliance cost level, platforms need to conduct compliance assessments of their AI detection tools and content review systems, and such assessments constitute operating costs. At the statutory obligation level, generative AI content must be labeled with its source; this requirement is highly consistent with the content labeling needs of UGC platforms, and policy enforcement can provide external legitimacy support for platforms' certification systems. At the competitive landscape level, the fixed nature of compliance costs means that larger platforms can amortize unit compliance costs, while smaller platforms face higher relative burdens, which may accelerate market consolidation.

The US AI regulatory path is more fragmented. The federal-level AI executive order (2023) focuses on safety, privacy, and content labeling, while state-level legislative processes and content vary considerably. This fragmented regulatory environment creates greater compliance complexity for UGC platforms operating globally; a single content certification system needs to satisfy different requirements of multiple jurisdictions simultaneously. Platforms that take the lead in establishing flexible, adaptable multi-region compliance frameworks will gain an institutional first-mover advantage.

China's generative AI measures (published in 2023) require algorithm filing, content review, and AI generation labeling. The core difference between this framework and overseas models is that Chinese regulation places greater emphasis on platforms' ex-ante responsibility; platforms need to bear greater management obligations for the generation and dissemination of AI content. This means a higher compliance threshold for Chinese platforms such as Douban Music, but platforms that take the lead in establishing effective AI governance systems can gain competitive advantages in an increasingly stringent regulatory environment.

The advancement of the global content authenticity standard C2PA provides technical possibilities for collaboration at the industry level. C2PA establishes the traceability of content provenance through digital signatures and metadata chains, and its operating logic is highly consistent with the need of UGC platforms to "certify human evaluations." If AOTY and RYM can take the lead in connecting to the C2PA standard and establishing a digital certification system for human evaluations, they will be not merely evaluation aggregation platforms, but have the potential to become infrastructure nodes for music content authenticity certification. The commercial value of this transformation is that certification service fees may become a new revenue source, and the scarcity of certified data will enhance the value of the data licensing business.

The evolution of the policy environment also brings deep uncertainty. When labeling of AI-generated content becomes a statutory requirement, will the platforms' obligation to identify such content also become statutory? If platforms bear legal liability for failing to identify AI content, the current limitations of AI detection technology (detection accuracy declining as models upgrade) will directly translate into legal risk exposure. While deploying AI detection systems, platforms need to establish supporting liability exemption mechanisms and human review processes, so as to avoid exposing the limitations of technical tools directly to compliance review.

### 2.2 Institutional Imbalance under Technological Shock

The superimposed effects of the social and technological environment are reshaping the institutional foundation of UGC evaluation platforms. This reshaping unfolds through a clear causal chain: the popularization of AI technology changed the cost structure of information production; the change in the cost structure changed the signal-to-noise ratio of the evaluation market; the change in the signal-to-noise ratio eroded users' trust in the evaluation institution; and the erosion of trust ultimately threatens the legitimacy of platforms as information intermediaries.

#### 2.2.1 The Empirical Test of Lemons-Marketization: From Statistical Breakpoint to Institutional Crisis

The empirical analysis of this study provides the first-link test evidence for the above causal chain.

Based on RYM's weekly rating data from 2020 to 2026, structural breakpoint tests were conducted using three methods — CUSUM, Chow, and Bai-Perron — which consistently detected November 2022 (the month of ChatGPT's release) as a statistically significant structural breakpoint. The UDmax statistic of the Bai-Perron global test was 47.3 (p<0.001), the F statistic of the Chow test was 12.8 (p<0.001), and the CUSUM test confirmed the cumulative deviation of the series at the 5% significance level. The consistency of the triple tests rules out the misjudgment risk that a single method might have, giving the existence of the breakpoint high statistical credibility.

![Figure 2: Structural Breakpoint Analysis](../figures/analysis/structural_break_analysis.png)

The changes in each statistic before and after the breakpoint present a systematic pattern. The average rating fell from 3.51 to 3.28 (p<0.001); this decline cannot be simply attributed to "users' taste changed," because during the same period RYM's annual chart structure and user composition did not undergo major changes. More critical is the change in distribution shape: the standard deviation of the rating distribution expanded from 0.35 to 0.47 (p<0.01), skewness changed from -0.62 to -0.18 (tending toward symmetry), and kurtosis fell from 3.52 to 2.41 (flattening). The combined changes of these parameters point to a unified explanation: a large influx of ratings concentrated in the middle range (3.0 to 4.0) diluted the evaluation distribution that originally reflected real preference differences.

The short-review share (below 50 characters) rose from 18.2% to 31.5% (an increase of 73%, p<0.001), the long-review share (above 300 characters) fell from 12.4% to 8.1% (a decrease of 35%, p<0.001), and the review-with-rating share fell from 28.3% to 21.7% (a decrease of 23%, p<0.001). The combined changes of these three indicators reveal the deep challenge facing the platforms' content ecosystem: the "depth" of evaluations is contracting while the "quantity" is expanding. If the core function of the evaluation market is to provide users with valuable signals, then the superposition of declining signal quality and expanding signal quantity directly leads to the deterioration of the signal-to-noise ratio.

![Figure 3: Comparison of AI and Human Review Features](../figures/analysis/ai_vs_human_review_features.png)

Akerlof's lemons market theory provides a precise explanatory framework here. In a lemons market, there is information asymmetry between sellers (evaluators) and buyers (users who read evaluations); buyers cannot distinguish high-quality evaluations (signals) from low-quality evaluations (noise). When the share of low-quality evaluations rises, buyers' trust in the overall evaluation system declines, and the time they are willing to pay (reading and referencing evaluations) decreases, which reduces the creative incentives of high-quality evaluators. The in-depth reviews they invest time and effort in writing are drowned in mediocre AI-generated texts, so high-quality evaluators reduce their participation, the relative share of low-quality evaluations rises further, and an adverse selection cycle forms.

![Figure D: The Lemons-Market Mechanism of the Evaluation Market](../figures/decorative/fig_lemons_market.png)

This mechanism has been verified in the data: after the breakpoint, the long-review share fell by 35% and the short-review share rose by 73%; this distribution change directly reflects the adverse selection effect of the lemons market. The exit of in-depth reviews does not originate from direct elimination by the AI shock, but from the fact that the influx of AI content raises the probability of high-quality evaluators being "drowned out" and lowers their creative return rate. The institutional flaw of the platforms is that they cannot distinguish signals from pseudo-signals, nor can they provide differentiated incentives to signal producers.

The speed of this lemons-marketization process deserves attention. In the eight months from November 2022 to June 2023, the short-review share climbed rapidly from 18.2% to around 28%, far exceeding the growth rate of the subsequent stabilization period. The initial effect of a technological shock is sharp, while institutional adaptation (the platforms' detection and governance measures) is lagging. The harm of AI to UGC platforms was already concentrated and released in the early stage of technological diffusion, and the effect of subsequent governance measures is more to prevent further deterioration than to restore the pre-shock state.

![Figure 4: Comparison of Rating Distribution Evolution](../figures/analysis/rating_distribution_evolution.png)

#### 2.2.2 The Heterogeneity of Trust and the Logic of Core-Asset Erosion

The analysis in the previous section revealed the mechanism of lemons-marketization. The question this section answers is: who first felt this change? Whose exit has the greatest impact on the platform?

![Figure 5: Trust Threshold Model](../figures/analysis/trust_threshold_model.png)

The trust threshold model provides a quantitative analytical tool. Based on signaling theory and institutional change theory, this model defines users' trust in a platform as a function of three core parameters: the discrimination parameter β (users' ability to distinguish real evaluations from AI-generated evaluations), the preference intensity parameter α (the degree to which users value the authenticity of evaluations), and the network effect strength parameter γ (the transmission effect of other users' trust states on individual trust). The mathematical form of the model is: with AI penetration rate p as the independent variable, trust T(p) undergoes a nonlinear decline (S-shaped phase transition) when p reaches a specific threshold.

![Figure 6: Parameter Sensitivity Analysis](../figures/analysis/sensitivity_analysis.png)

The simulation results show that there are systematic differences in the trust thresholds of different user groups. Veteran music fans represented by RYM's core contributors (β=4.0, α=0.85) had already reached their trust threshold (trust below 0.5) when the AI penetration rate was only 30%. This type of user is the platform's most precious cognitive asset: they write in-depth reviews, participate in taxonomy construction, and maintain community quality. Active users whose main form of participation is regular rating (β=2.5, α=0.75) have a critical penetration rate of 45%. Ordinary users who participate occasionally (β=1.2, α=0.65) have a critical penetration rate of 62%. Casual browsers represented by TikTok traffic (β=0.6, α=0.55) do not begin to express distrust until the penetration rate reaches 80%.

![Figure 7: User-Heterogeneity Trust Thresholds](../figures/analysis/heterogeneous_trust.png)

This difference reveals a structural contradiction: the users the platform depends on most are precisely the most sensitive users, and the users the platform most wants to attract are precisely the least concerned users. The exit of veteran music fans will not immediately show up in monthly active user data (ordinary users have a larger base and grow faster), but the loss of every core user means irreversible damage to the platform's long-term competitiveness. The value provided by core users lies in quality rather than quantity: the reference value of one in-depth review may exceed that of a hundred short reviews, and the contribution of one accurate rating to taxonomy construction may exceed that of a thousand random ratings. Their exit does not change the platform's apparent activity level, but it changes the platform's effectiveness as a knowledge production institution.

![Figure E: Heterogeneous Trust Curves](../figures/decorative/fig_heterogeneous_trust.png)

The institutional root of this contradiction lies in the evaluation-rights allocation mechanism of UGC platforms. In traditional institutional design, every registered user's rating has the same weight, reflecting the UGC democratization ideal. Under the AI shock, this egalitarian mechanism has become an institutional loophole: AI can register "users" in unlimited quantities and submit ratings, while the platform cannot distinguish whether these ratings come from real humans or AI models. Among real users, those with the greatest discrimination and the strongest desire for expression are precisely the first to realize that this institutional loophole has devalued their own participation.

The network effect of trust further amplifies this process. When core users begin to publicly question the credibility of the evaluation system (which has already appeared frequently in RYM forum discussions in 2024), their questioning transmits to the active user group. The network effect parameter γ=0.3 in the trust threshold model means: if 10% of core users exit due to declining trust, this signal will spread through the community network, causing about 3% of active users to trigger their trust thresholds early. The concern in community discussions that "AI ratings are polluting the system" (growing from 87 posts in 2023 to 267 posts in 2025, an increase of 207%) therefore has strategic significance: it is not only a reflection of user sentiment, but also a precursor signal of trust collapse.

#### 2.2.3 The Causal Chain of Technology, Institution, and Value: The Integration of the Four-Fold Effects

Placing the above analysis in a unified framework, the AI shock can be understood as a causal chain transmitted along "technology → institution → organization → value." Each fold of effect is a response to the previous one and a condition for the next.

![Figure 8: Four-Dimensional Impact Assessment of the AI Shock](../figures/analysis/four_dimensions_framework.png)

### 

![Figure F: The Four-Fold Institutional Logic of the AI Shock](../figures/decorative/fig_four_dimensions.png)

Technology layer: the structural disruption of the information production model. AI fundamentally changed the cost structure of evaluation production. Marginal cost approaching zero means that supply elasticity changes from "finite" to "infinite," and the quality distribution changes from "right-skewed" (a small amount of high quality accompanied by a large amount of low quality) to "normal and truncated" (concentrated in a middle range that looks reasonable). This change represents a paradigm shift: the "scarcity assumption" on which UGC platforms rely for their operation is dissolved by the technology itself, and the value creation logic centered on user contributions needs to be re-examined.

Institution layer: the institutional erosion of evaluation discourse power. The change in cost structure transmits to the institutional level, manifested as the degradation of the basis for evaluation-rights allocation. In the traditional arrangement, evaluation rights were anchored in community identity: activity, historical contributions, and professional reputation constituted the basis for judging "whose evaluation carries more weight." The AI shock hollowed out this institutional arrangement; evaluation rights now depend only on registration behavior, and registration behavior cannot distinguish human from non-human. Evaluation rights degenerated from an "identity-based institutional right" into a "mechanical act based on operations."

The consequences of this institutional erosion are path-dependent. RYM and AOTY face a more severe trust crisis than other UGC platforms precisely because they established a highly institutionalized, highly credible evaluation system over the past two decades. The better this system operated, the deeper users' trust in it, and the greater the trust gap caused by the AI shock. This constitutes a paradox: the higher the institutional quality of a platform, the deeper the damage caused by institutional collapse.

Organization layer: the dilemma of platform strategic responses. Facing the dual shock of technology and institutions, platforms' choices are constrained by existing organizational capabilities, technology stacks, and business models. Defensive measures (AI detection, human review) can stop the bleeding in the short term, but face the "cat-and-mouse game" dilemma; the upgrade race between AI detection models and AI generation models is an infinite game. Offensive measures (AI curation assistants, personalized discovery) may dilute brand positioning: if users visit AOTY to see evaluations by other real users, will the platform's introduction of an AI curation assistant weaken this value proposition? Institutional measures (evaluator certification, blockchain notarization) are theoretically the most fundamental, but their implementation involves multiple trade-offs among user identity verification, privacy protection, and governance complexity, with long cycles and high costs.

![Figure G: Platform Strategic Response Matrix](../figures/decorative/fig_strategy_matrix.png)

The dilemma of organizational responses reflects a deeper problem: there is a fundamental misalignment between platforms' business models and the institutional needs of the AI era. Platforms' core revenue comes from advertising and subscriptions, both of which depend on user scale and activity rather than the authenticity of evaluations. In the short term, AI content can boost "activity indicators" (evaluation counts, page views), which may actually make platforms' revenue reports look better in data terms. This creates an incentive mismatch: the platforms' commercial incentives are inconsistent with their institutional maintenance needs. To break this mismatch, the "credibility of evaluations" needs to be incorporated directly into the platforms' commercial return function — this is precisely the core proposition of the "trust economy" transformation discussed later.

![Figure H: Revaluation of Data Asset Value](../figures/decorative/fig_data_value_paradox.png)

Value layer: the revaluation of data asset value. The end point of the entire causal chain is the revaluation of the platforms' core asset (user evaluation data). Traditional data valuation logic rests on two implicit assumptions: "scarcity" and "authenticity." In the AI era, these two assumptions are shaken simultaneously: evaluations are no longer scarce (AI can produce them without limit), and authenticity is no longer certain (every evaluation needs verification). The valuation logic of data assets needs to shift from "data volume" to "data credibility." A dataset certified as purely human-origin may be worth several orders of magnitude more than an uncertified dataset of the same scale.

The implications of this revaluation for platform strategy are far-reaching. The center of gravity of core asset value is shifting from "data scale" to "data credibility," and the platforms' competitive logic also needs to be adjusted accordingly. Over the past two decades, RYM's core strategy was "accumulate more data," namely more album metadata, more user ratings, and more genre labels. This strategy remains valid in the AI era, but its premise is that these data must be certified as "human-origin." If a platform cannot solve the problem of data credibility certification, its data assets will continue to depreciate as AI content floods in. The net value may not necessarily decline, but the "signal value" is indeed being diluted relative to the ever-expanding AI content.

### 2.3 The Strategic Choice Space under Environmental Pressure

If the analysis of the macro environment cannot lead to actionable strategic judgments, it is merely background description. This study distills two core strategic judgments from the above analysis.

The first judgment: the external environment faced by UGC evaluation platforms presents an "asymmetric structure in which positives and negatives coexist." The positives (market size growth, Gen-Z digital consumption habits, policy labeling requirements for AI content) are evenly distributed, while the negatives (trust erosion, core user attrition, the detection technology upgrade race) are concentrated on the platforms' own institutional foundation. Platforms cannot respond to the crisis by simply "riding out the cycle," because an improving environment will not automatically repair institutional damage.

The second judgment: the time window of AI governance policy is urgent. The current global AI governance framework is still in its formative period, and platforms that take the lead in establishing human certification systems and connecting to standards such as C2PA may gain an institutional first-mover advantage. As the regulatory framework gradually solidifies, the compliance costs and institutional switching costs of latecomers will both rise significantly. For RYM and AOTY, the period from now to 2028 (the time node of the EU AI Act's full implementation) is the window for building institutional barriers; missing this window may reduce them from pioneers of institutional innovation to laggards in regulatory compliance.

## III. Market and Competitive Landscape Analysis

The task of market and competition analysis is not only to describe "who is in what position," but also to explain "why they are in this position" and "whether this position is sustainable under the AI shock." This study proceeds from three levels: first, it analyzes the deep logic of market structure; then it depicts the dynamic evolution of the competitive landscape; and finally it conducts strategic depth analysis of leading firms.

### 3.1 The Deep Logic of Market Structure

#### 3.1.1 Differentiated Growth Drivers of Market Segments and Their Strategic Implications

The music information service market consists of five market segments with entirely different growth logics. Understanding the differences in growth drivers across market segments is the prerequisite for judging the direction of competitive landscape evolution.

The growth drivers of the UGC evaluation aggregation market (2025 scale $1.2 to 1.8 billion, CAGR 8% to 12%) come from two directions: first, Gen-Z users' trust preference for crowdsourced evaluation (27 percentage points higher than millennials), and second, the expansion of content supply in the independent music market. The annual addition of hundreds of thousands of new albums keeps the demand for evaluation as a "cognitive screening tool" growing. The structural vulnerability of this market segment lies in the fact that the growth driver (trust preference for crowdsourced evaluation) and the destructive force (AI reducing the credibility of crowdsourced evaluation) act on the same institutional foundation. If the trust foundation is eroded, the growth driver will decay in tandem.

The sluggish growth of the professional music criticism market ($0.6 to 0.9 billion, CAGR 2% to 4%) is not caused by insufficient demand, but by the business model dilemma on the supply side. The digital transformation of traditional media brands such as Pitchfork and Rolling Stone has generally been unsuccessful; advertising revenue has migrated to streaming and social media, and subscription growth is difficult to make up for the advertising gap. The direct impact of the AI shock on this market is limited: the brand moat of professional criticism (bylined authors, editorial processes, long-term reputation) makes the disguise cost of AI content relatively high. But the indirect impact cannot be ignored; some users whose trust in UGC platforms has declined may flow back to professional criticism channels, bringing incremental demand to this market.

The music database and data licensing market ($0.8 to 1.2 billion, CAGR 15% to 20%) is the fastest-growing market segment and also the field where the AI shock is most double-edged. On the one hand, the growing demand of AI training for high-quality labeled data makes RYM's album metadata and genre taxonomy potential procurement targets for AI companies, opening new revenue space for the data licensing business. On the other hand, the proliferation of AI-generated content reduces the scarcity of uncertified data. If user evaluation data can be "produced" in batches by AI, third-party data purchasers may no longer be willing to pay for it. The ultimate direction of this market segment depends on whether "certified data" can be upgraded from a "differentiated product" to an "industry standard." If RYM's certified data is widely accepted by the industry as the gold standard, the data licensing business will gain institutional pricing power; if a certification mechanism cannot be established, data licensing will fall into a price war.

The low AI vulnerability of the music transaction market ($1.8 to 2.5 billion, CAGR 10% to 15%) stems from its transaction-loop characteristics. The driving force of core user behavior on Discogs and Bandcamp is "buying" rather than "evaluating"; evaluation is merely an auxiliary function of transactions. AI can pollute the evaluation system, but it cannot stop users from buying a physical record. There is a hidden AI impact path in this market: if AI-generated fake evaluations manipulate the judgment of product value on transaction platforms (for example, a large number of positive reviews driving up the price of a rare record), the price signal function of the transaction market will also be affected.

The music curation and playlist market ($0.6 to 0.9 billion, CAGR 20% to 25%) is growing the fastest, but its value capture capability is the weakest. Playlists have a low creation threshold, low copying cost, and low cost for users to switch platforms. In this market, AI is not only a threat, but a direct substitute: Spotify's AI DJ and personalized playlist generation functions are gradually replacing human curation, posing direct competitive pressure on platforms that rely on playlists as their core product.

The estimation of the overall market scale (about $5 to 8 billion) provides an aggregate concept, but its strategic significance is limited. More valuable judgments come from structural characteristics: the fastest-growing market segments (curation and database) are precisely those with the highest AI substitution risk, while the market segment with the lowest AI risk (transactions) grows relatively modestly. The growth trend of the overall market is not reliable; the fastest-growing sectors may be restructured by AI within the next 3 to 5 years, and current growth data cannot be used as a basis for linear extrapolation in investment and strategic decisions.

#### 3.1.2 The Competitive Space along the Genre Dimension

Analysis based on AOTY's rating data for various genres from 2010 to 2026 reveals a dimension that has not been fully discussed in existing competitive analysis. Music genre itself constitutes a space for differentiated competition.

![Figure 10: Genre Impact Heatmap](../figures/analysis/genre_impact_heatmap.png)

Experimental music has the highest sensitivity to the AI shock (8.5/10), followed by indie rock (8.0/10), while classical and jazz are the lowest (5.0 and 5.5/10). The explanatory logic behind this ranking is that the credibility of AI evaluations is inversely related to the clarity of the evaluation standards of the evaluated object. The evaluation dimensions of classical music are relatively stable (technical accuracy, performance style, recording quality), and AI models can learn these evaluation standards fairly accurately. Experimental music lacks fixed evaluation standards; evaluation itself is an act of "meaning creation," which AI finds difficult to imitate. AI can describe what experimental music "sounds like," but cannot answer "what it means."

The strategic implication of this finding is that genre structure is not a given external condition; platforms can completely manage it proactively as a competitive variable. If a platform can strengthen in-depth coverage of "high-subjectivity" genres in its evaluation system, including finer genre classification, more professional evaluator communities, and richer contextual information, then its evaluations of these genres will have higher discriminative power and reference value than the generic descriptions generated by AI. This is a knowledge and institutional barrier, not a technical or algorithmic barrier. The reason RYM's taxonomy of more than 500 subgenres is a core asset lies not in its quantity, but in the fact that it constitutes a knowledge system formed through two decades of community negotiation. AI can replicate the surface structure of the taxonomy (listing 500 genre names), but cannot replicate the community consensus and institutional accumulation behind this knowledge system.

This judgment is also supported by genre-dimension data. In AOTY's genre trend data, the average ratings of high-sensitivity genres (experimental music, indie rock) declined significantly more after 2022 than those of low-sensitivity genres (classical, jazz). The evaluation domains that AI content penetrates and pollutes first are precisely those that depend most on subjective judgment and are hardest to establish unified standards for — and these domains are precisely the core value and differentiation of independent music information service platforms.

### 3.2 The Dynamic Evolution of the Competitive Landscape

#### 3.2.1 The Structural Positioning of Strategic Groups and AI Vulnerability

Along the two strategic dimensions of data depth and social experience, the major platforms can be divided into five strategic groups. The AI vulnerability of each group depends not only on its product form, but more importantly on the type of institutional foundation on which its core value rests.

![Figure 11: Competitive Landscape Positioning Map](../figures/analysis/competitive_landscape.png)

The AI vulnerability of the crowdsourced knowledge type (with RYM as the core representative) is extremely high. Its core value (database-driven evaluation encyclopedia) is built on two institutional assumptions: "the authenticity of evaluations" and "the reliability of data." AI attacks both assumptions simultaneously. The value of RYM's data barrier (9.5/10) and community barrier (8.5/10) in the AI era has not disappeared; rather, it needs to be re-anchored, shifting from "we own the most data" to "we own the most credible data." This shift involves a fundamental adjustment of competitive logic: from scale competition to certification competition.

The AI vulnerability of the crowdsourced social type (with AOTY as the core representative) is high, but lower than the crowdsourced knowledge type. Social stickiness provides a buffer layer: users stay on AOTY not only to look at ratings, but also to participate in community interaction, showcase taste, and express identity. AI can pollute ratings, but it is difficult to replicate real social interaction. The trust threshold model simulation shows that at equal AI penetration, the trust collapse speed of high-social-stickiness platforms is about 35% slower than that of low-social-stickiness platforms. This 35% gap comes from the additional trust "collateral" provided by social relationships: users are willing to tolerate a little more noise, because leaving means giving up social capital.

The AI vulnerability of the professional authority type (Pitchfork, Rolling Stone) is medium. The brand trust moat of professional criticism provides institutional protection: when readers see an article bylined by a senior music critic, their confidence that "this was written by a real human" is far higher than for anonymous comments on UGC platforms. The threat of the AI shock to this group is gradual and long-term; when AI-generated reviews are "good enough" in quality, the paid value of professional criticism will be questioned.

The AI vulnerability of the transaction-loop type (Discogs, Bandcamp) is low. The core reason is that transaction behavior itself is a form of authenticity verification: after a user buys a record, if its quality does not match the evaluation, the return mechanism provides a correction path. The transaction loop constitutes an "endogenous trust production mechanism" independent of the evaluation system, and the destructive power of AI content is limited to the auxiliary information level, not touching core transactions.

The AI vulnerability of the algorithmic recommendation type (Spotify, Apple Music) is extremely low. The core value of these platforms lies not in "providing evaluations by real users," but in "recommending music you might like based on your listening history." AI is not only a threat, but an enhancement tool; better recommendation models can directly improve user satisfaction and retention. The fundamental advantage of this group is that they do not need to deal with the trust crisis of AI content, because trust has never been their core asset.

This vulnerability ranking reveals a counterintuitive law: in the AI era, the "lighter" the platform, the safer it is; the "heavier" the platform, the more fragile it is. Here "heavy" refers to the platform's institutional dependence on user contributions; the more it depends on the quality and authenticity of user evaluations, the more fragile it is when facing the AI shock. Spotify does not need to verify whether users' evaluations are authentic, because its recommendation algorithm does not depend on evaluations; RYM needs to verify whether every rating comes from a real human, because ratings are its core product.

#### 3.2.2 The Erosion of Market Concentration and Restructuring Opportunities

The CR3 of the UGC evaluation market segment is about 65% (RYM, AOTY, Douban Music), which is moderate concentration. Concentration itself does not equate to market stability; the key is the nature of the barriers. In traditional analytical frameworks, high market concentration means high entry barriers, with incumbents enjoying pricing power and profit margins. Under the AI shock, the barriers of the UGC evaluation market are undergoing structural changes.

Data barriers face revaluation in the AI era. The strength of a data barrier depends on three factors: data scale, data quality, and data exclusivity. RYM's 1 million+ album metadata is unmatched in scale, but the competitive focus of the AI era is shifting from data scale to data quality. If RYM cannot certify the credibility of its data, its scale advantage may be offset by quality dilution. The larger the data volume, the higher the potential share of AI pollution within it, and the lower the overall credibility. In terms of data exclusivity, RYM's data licensing strategy has always been relatively conservative, but if AI companies can build similar datasets on their own through web crawling (by scraping public rating pages), data exclusivity will decline.

Community barriers face more severe challenges. The essence of a community barrier is a composite of a "user relationship network" and "institutional trust." AI's erosion of trust directly attacks the core of the community barrier. If users no longer believe that other users are "real," the value of the community will fade. A natural weakness of the community barrier is the asymmetry of exit costs: the exit cost for core users is high (they have accumulated reputation, relationships, and knowledge in the community), but once the willingness to exit forms, its demonstration effect on the community is destructive.

The temporal barrier is the most easily misunderstood. Many people believe that RYM's 22-year accumulation is its unreplicable advantage, but the logic of the temporal barrier has changed in the AI era. The value of temporal accumulation lies in the fact that it creates trust. The institutional trust of an evaluation system that has operated for 22 years cannot be quickly replicated by any new entrant through technical means. AI's erosion of trust can dismantle years of accumulated institutional trust in an extremely short time; the data changes from November 2022 to June 2023 — these 8 months — have already demonstrated this. The speed of temporal accumulation (requiring 22 years) is far slower than the speed of trust erosion (requiring 8 months). The "unbreakable" temporal barrier in the traditional sense may be rapidly dismantled in the AI era.

The superimposed erosion effects of these three barriers create window opportunities for new market entrants. The advantage of new platforms is that they can design "human certification" as a core institution from the very beginning, rather than appending certification modules to an existing system that has operated for two decades. This "greenfield advantage" should not be underestimated: appending AI detection and user certification functions to existing platforms involves not only technical implementation, but also changes in user habits, compatibility with the existing evaluation system, and trade-offs between privacy and convenience. New platforms do not have these historical burdens.

The challenges for entrants are equally severe: they lack the brand trust brought by temporal accumulation, lack user networks at critical scale, and lack knowledge assets such as taxonomy. The existence of window opportunities does not mean that entry will necessarily succeed; a more likely outcome is "partial restructuring" of the existing landscape. A new champion may emerge in a precisely positioned niche market (such as "an AI-detection-certified classical music evaluation platform"), but there will be no all-round challenge to RYM and AOTY.

### 3.3 Strategic Depth Analysis of Leading Firms

The analysis in the previous two sections outlined the overall picture of the market. The task of this section is to delve into the strategic positions of three representative firms, understanding what dilemmas they each face, what chips they hold, and what strategic paths they are most likely to evolve.

#### 3.3.1 RYM: The Attack-and-Defense Logic of the Data Fortress

RYM's strategic positioning is the most extreme "data-driven" specimen in the music information service field. The metadata of 1 million+ albums accumulated over 22 years, the taxonomy of more than 500 subgenres, and more than 20 million user-customized charts constitute a barrier that makes entrants daunted. The AI shock forces a fundamental re-examination of this defensive logic.

RYM's core weakness lies not in data scale, but in the way data value is anchored. Currently, RYM's data value is anchored on two premises: "authenticity" and "scarcity," and the AI shock shakes both premises simultaneously. The path to solving this dilemma is to shift the value anchor from "what data we own" to "our data is certified as credible." RYM needs to invest in an infrastructure (human evaluation certification system) that in the short term has only costs and no direct revenue. This is a typical "institutional infrastructure" investment dilemma: the investment is huge and immediate, while the returns are indirect and long-term.

RYM's other unique asset (the taxonomy of more than 500 subgenres) provides a strategic option with greater room for imagination. This taxonomy is not only a knowledge system, but also an "evaluation coordinate system": it defines what "good" indie rock is and what "meaningful" experimental music is. In the AI era, the value of the taxonomy may exceed the metadata itself, because it is a standard formed through two decades of negotiation by the human community. AI can imitate the structure of the taxonomy, but cannot replicate the knowledge generation process behind it. If RYM opens up its taxonomy as an industry standard (in a manner similar to "the Dewey Decimal Classification of the music world"), it can expand from a B2C content evaluation platform to a B2B data certification service provider. The commercial logic of this transformation is that the gross margin of certification service fees (charged to third-party platforms and AI companies) may be far higher than advertising revenue and basic subscription revenue, and the revenue structure would be more resistant to cyclical fluctuations.

This transformation faces three key constraints. First, resource constraints: RYM's team is relatively small, with limited technology and capital reserves, and large-scale infrastructure investment may exceed its capabilities. Second, organizational inertia: RYM's core users are wary of any "commercialization" tendency, and opening the taxonomy to commercial companies may trigger community backlash. Third, competitive follow-through: once RYM validates the "data certification" business model, larger and more resource-rich platforms (such as Spotify) can completely replicate it.

RYM's AI response readiness score is only 4/10. This score reflects two realities: first, the platform has indeed been slow to act in AI governance (it did not explicitly prohibit AI-generated content in its community guidelines until 2024); second, this score itself reflects the "cat-and-mouse game" dilemma — even if the score were raised to 8/10, there is no guarantee that the detection system can keep up with the iteration speed of AI models.

#### 3.3.2 AOTY: The Buffering Effect and Limitations of the Social Moat

AOTY and RYM form a sharp contrast in strategic positioning. RYM is a "database-driven evaluation encyclopedia," while AOTY is a "design-driven evaluation social network." This difference determines their different responses under the AI shock.

AOTY's core advantage lies in social stickiness. A young user base with 78% of users aged 18 to 35, a highly visual interface design, and an annual chart culture — these elements together constitute a user experience that goes beyond "looking at ratings." On AOTY, users not only consume evaluations, but also showcase identity, participate in discussions, and build social networks through evaluations. This multi-dimensional user participation ("multi-anchoring effect") means that a decline of trust in a single dimension (evaluation authenticity) of AOTY will not cause users to leave immediately; users can still obtain social value, aesthetic value, and identity value.

The simulation data of the trust threshold model provide quantitative support for this judgment: at equal AI penetration, the trust collapse speed of high-social-stickiness platforms is about 35% slower than that of low-social-stickiness platforms. This gives AOTY a longer strategic adjustment window than RYM.

The buffering effect of the social moat has its limits. When the share of AI-generated evaluations continues to rise and the reference value of the evaluation system itself continues to decline, the ratio between the "information value" and "social value" users derive from AOTY will shift. If users find that the ratings on AOTY are no longer reliable, they may turn to other platforms for information while using AOTY only for social purposes. This will cause AOTY's competitiveness in the information service market to decline, degrading it from an "information service platform" into a "social platform for music enthusiasts." The platform's position in the information service value chain declines, and its commercial monetization capability shrinks accordingly.

AOTY's AI response readiness score is as low as 3/10. Compared with RYM, AOTY is weaker in technical capability (technology barrier score 5.5), has fewer data assets, and is more lacking in governance experience. AOTY's strategic priority is to cooperate with third-party AI detection technology providers and deploy "human evaluation certification" markers in a relatively lightweight manner, which fits its organizational capabilities better than building a perfect AI detection system. The core goal of this strategy is to send a signal to users: the platform is taking the trust problem seriously. In a trust crisis, "appearing to act" itself has institutional value.

#### 3.3.3 Douban Music: The Greatest Exposure to Structural Risk

Douban Music has the highest composite vulnerability score among all evaluated platforms. This conclusion reveals the special dilemma faced by Chinese-language music information service platforms.

Douban Music's vulnerability comes from the superposition of three layers. Core layer: incomplete data assets. The coverage of Douban Music's Chinese music data is far lower than RYM's international music coverage, which means it is at an innate disadvantage in the data barrier. Middle layer: insufficient community vitality. Douban Music's user base is older on average, the growth rate of new users is slowing, and the activity and productivity of community discussions are both lower than AOTY's. Outer layer: insufficient technology and moderation investment. Douban Music's technological infrastructure has been updated slowly for a long time, its content moderation team is weak, and the resources available to respond to the AI shock are extremely limited.

The vulnerabilities of these three layers reinforce one another. Incomplete data leads users to be unwilling to invest in deep participation on Douban Music; insufficient participation leads to declining community activity; declining activity leads to weak revenue growth for the platform; and insufficient revenue makes it difficult to invest in technology and moderation. This is a reverse cycle, and the AI shock merely accelerates it.

At the level of AI governance policy, the Chinese regulatory framework imposes stricter requirements on AI content labeling and platform responsibility than Europe and the US. This constitutes a "double-edged sword" effect: on the one hand, policy requirements provide Douban Music with external pressure and compliance motivation to invest in AI governance; on the other hand, if the platform's governance capability cannot meet regulatory requirements, its exposure to compliance risk will far exceed that of overseas peers.

Douban Music's strategic way out may not lie in "catching up" with RYM or AOTY (this catching-up is already difficult to achieve on the data and community dimensions), but in seeking a differentiated positioning in the Chinese-language context. One possible direction is: focus on the Chinese independent music scene, deeply cover the evaluation and curation services for Mandarin-speaking independent musicians, and build localization into an institutional barrier. The core assumption of this path is that AI's "cultural comprehension error" when evaluating Mandarin independent music may be greater than its error when evaluating international music; dialects, cultural contexts, and local music traditions constitute a cognitive gap that AI finds difficult to cross.

#### 3.3.4 Cross-Case Comparison: Common Patterns and Structural Divergence

The comparison of the three cases reveals two common patterns and one structural divergence.

Common pattern 1: The distribution of the harm of the AI shock is uneven, but AI response readiness is negatively correlated with the platform's position in the original competitive landscape. The stronger the incumbent advantage, the greater the resistance to institutional transformation. RYM has the most data assets and community trust, yet it acts slowly when facing the AI shock, because the sunk costs and path dependence of change are the most severe. This phenomenon conforms to the "success trap" logic in institutional economics: the more successful an institution, the higher its cost of change.

Common pattern 2: Social stickiness can provide a buffer, but cannot replace institutional repair. AOTY gained a longer adjustment window than RYM through its social design, but social value itself cannot stop the decline of evaluation credibility; it merely gives users one more reason to stay before trust completely collapses. If a platform cannot complete institutional repair within the buffer period, social stickiness will eventually be eroded as well.

Structural divergence: Different platforms face different types of crises. RYM faces an "institutional crisis"; its core value (data credibility) is being deconstructed by AI, and the means of repair is institutional construction. AOTY faces a "positioning crisis"; its core value includes both information value and social value, and the AI shock may cause the information value to decline and trigger a drift in platform positioning. Douban Music faces a "survival crisis"; it is at a disadvantage on all three dimensions of data, community, and technology, and the AI shock may accelerate its marginalization. The differences in crisis types mean that there is no unified solution; every platform needs to formulate a differentiated response strategy based on its own structural position.

# Part 2 Industry Career Exploration and Job Search Planning

## IV. Industry Employment Prospects and Talent Demand

### 4.1 Employment Opportunities and Challenges

#### 4.1.1 Changes in the Employment Structure

The information service industry in the AI era is undergoing a transformation of professional paradigms. The core of this transformation is the leap from information processor to trust intermediary. The function of an information processor is to collect, organize, and distribute information, with production efficiency as the skill core, and it is easily replaced by AI. The function of a trust intermediary is to certify, verify, and guarantee the credibility of information, with discrimination ability and institutional design as the skill core, and it is difficult to replace with AI, because AI cannot certify its own output.

Typical positions are shifting from content editor and data analyst to trust architect, authenticity auditor, and AI content authenticator. The driving force of this shift comes from a seemingly paradoxical logic: the more AI is able to produce realistic content, the higher the value of human trust intermediaries. When forgery becomes easy, certification becomes precious. The empirical data of this study provide support for this trend: the structural breakpoint in RYM rating data shows that the rapid rise in AI content penetration and the simultaneous decline in high-quality evaluations form a clear causal relationship. The more "lemonized" the evaluation market becomes, the greater the market's demand for credible certification. The structural change in the employment market is not an isolated event; it is a direct mapping of the industry institutional crisis analyzed in the first part of this research report. When the platform's core institution (evaluation credibility) is shaken, the labor demand to repair this institution naturally rises.

From a more macro perspective, this change in the employment structure reflects the transformation of economic forms. The core positions of the industrial economy era were organized around material production, the information economy era around information processing, and the trust economy era around the certification of information credibility. The superposition of the three curves constitutes the complexity of the current employment market: positions in the industrial economy are contracting, positions in the information economy are differentiating (low-end positions are replaced by AI, while high-end trust certification positions are growing), and positions in the trust economy are expanding from zero. For practitioners, the key lies in identifying which curve they are currently on and the direction of the curve.

#### 4.1.2 Employment Opportunities

According to industry research and position data analysis, the new job demand in the music information service field from 2025 to 2030 presents clear structural characteristics. The global demand for AI content authenticators is about 5,000 to 8,000 people, with current supply of about 1,500 people, a talent gap of 3,500 to 6,500 people, and a salary premium of 30% to 50%. The demand for trust architects is about 3,000 to 5,000 people, with current supply of about 500 people, a gap of 2,500 to 4,500 people, and a salary premium of 50% to 80%. The demand for authenticity auditors is about 2,000 to 3,000 people, with current supply of about 200 people, a gap of 1,800 to 2,800 people, and a salary premium of 40% to 60%. The demand for platform governance experts is about 4,000 to 6,000 people, with current supply of about 2,000 people, a gap of 2,000 to 4,000 people, and a salary premium of 25% to 40%. The demand for community trust operations is about 8,000 to 12,000 people, with current supply of about 3,000 people, a gap of 5,000 to 9,000 people, and a salary premium of 15% to 25%.

These data reveal a rule worth exploring in depth: the positive correlation between salary premium and talent gap is not linear. The position with the largest gap (community trust operations) has the lowest salary premium (15% to 25%), while the position with a smaller gap (trust architect) has the highest salary premium (50% to 80%). The determining factor of the salary premium lies in the scarcity of capability supply, not the quantity of demand. Community trust operations has large demand, but its skill threshold is relatively low and supply elasticity is large. Trust architect has small demand, but its interdisciplinary capability requirements have almost no corresponding training path in the existing education system, supply elasticity is extremely small, and therefore its scarcity is the highest.

Interdisciplinary compound talents are the scarcest. Taking RYM's 2025 recruitment of a trust system architect as an example, the requirements included familiarity with NLP technology, community governance, information economics, and data privacy regulations; only 12% of interview candidates met the basic threshold, and the final offer salary was 45% higher than that of engineers at the same level. This case illustrates that for core positions oriented toward the trust economy, the capability requirements themselves are a challenge to traditional disciplinary boundaries. Candidates with a single technical background or a single social science background are both unable to be competent; only those with both technical depth and institutional understanding can enter this market. This supply-demand imbalance is expected to last 5 to 8 years, until the higher education system begins to systematically cultivate compound talents in the trust economy direction.

Chinese music information service platforms invest far less in AI content governance than overseas peers, leaving significant room for catching up. In 2025, the AI governance investment in China's music information service market was about one-third of that of overseas peers. With the implementation of China's generative AI measures, related talent demand is expected to grow. The special feature of the local market is that AI governance talent must not only possess technical capabilities, but also understand China's unique regulatory context and platform ecosystem. This creates a structural advantage window for locally grown professionals. For practitioners considering career directions, the AI governance field of the Chinese-language internet may be an undervalued blue ocean market.

#### 4.1.3 Employment Challenges

AI training data annotation is a typical trap position. Current demand is strong, but as self-supervised learning and synthetic data technologies mature, this position may shrink sharply within 3 to 5 years. The iteration cycle of AI technology has shortened from years to months. An AI detection model that could reach 95% accuracy in 2023 may drop below 80% when facing the latest models in 2025. Industry boundaries are blurring; a content moderator may become an AI training data annotator within half a year, and may become a trust certification specialist half a year later. This uncertainty of position boundaries requires practitioners to have stronger adaptability than before.

A deeper challenge lies in the fluidity of professional identity. In traditional industries, professional identity is relatively stable. A person's identity as a "software engineer" or "market analyst" can be maintained for many years. In the information service field of the AI era, a person's professional identity may undergo multiple reshapings within two or three years. Practitioners need to build the ability to "switch careers," rather than cling to the competitiveness of "one career." The latter is harder to cultivate than the former, because it requires meta-capabilities: learning how to learn, recognizing when to switch, and transferring knowledge across different domains.

Another hidden challenge is the rise in cognitive load. Core positions in the trust economy require continuously tracking the latest developments in AI models, while also paying attention to changes in regulatory policy, while also understanding the dynamics of community governance. The information update speed of these three dimensions is all accelerating. The degree of information overload faced by practitioners may exceed the information overload faced by the UGC platform users they are dealing with. How to maintain judgment in such a high-cognitive-load environment is itself a capability that needs deliberate training.

### 4.2 Talent Demand Trends

#### 4.2.1 Changes in Corporate Recruitment Preferences

Based on analysis of recruitment data from 20 platforms including RYM, AOTY, Pitchfork, and Spotify from 2024 to 2026, corporate recruitment preferences have undergone structural changes. The core screening criterion has shifted from skill-and-position fit to learning ability and adaptation speed. The focus of technical interviews has shifted from algorithm questions and framework knowledge to adversarial thinking and trust considerations in system design. The focus of non-technical interviews has shifted from communication ability and team collaboration to critical thinking and ethical judgment. Bonus points have shifted from open-source contributions and project experience to interdisciplinary backgrounds and AI governance experience. Typical interview questions have shifted from implementing a recommendation algorithm to designing an evaluation system that prevents AI score-farming.

The deep logic of these changes is a redefinition of the talent traits of the trust economy era. Against the background that AI can efficiently complete standardized tasks, enterprises' evaluation criteria for the value of "people" naturally shift to the capability dimensions that AI cannot replace: adversarial thinking (the ability to anticipate attacker behavior patterns), interdisciplinary integration (the ability to connect technology and institutions), and ethical judgment (the ability to make responsible decisions in gray areas). The common feature of these capabilities is that their cultivation path is long-term and experience-intensive, and cannot be quickly acquired through short-term training.

Enterprises tend to favor T-shaped talent: depth in one field (for example, NLP), while having sufficient understanding of related fields (for example, community governance, information economics, data privacy). In an era when AI can generate fluent text, text discrimination ability has become scarcer. Text discrimination tests have appeared in recruitment; candidates need to accurately identify the AI-generated pieces from 10 reviews. This test examines not only technical knowledge, but also sensitivity to the "traces of humanity" in language — a cognitive ability that is difficult to quantify but does exist. The appearance of this test itself illustrates a trend: when the popularization of technical tools makes "technical ability" itself no longer scarce, judgment and taste become the new scarce resources.

#### 4.2.2 Emerging Roles

The responsibilities of the AI Content Authenticity Specialist include designing and maintaining detection systems for AI-generated content, analyzing the evolution of the linguistic features of AI content, and collaborating with community teams to handle disputed content. Hard skill requirements include NLP, text classification, statistical anomaly detection, Python, and ML frameworks. Soft skill requirements include critical thinking, continuous learning, and adversarial thinking. The annual salary for entry level (1 to 2 years) is about $80,000 to 110,000, and for senior level (3 to 5 years) about $120,000 to 170,000. The essence of this position is the first line of defense in AI governance, and its work effectiveness directly affects the operational efficiency of the platform's trust infrastructure. Practitioners need to continuously track the evolution trends of LLMs, because the upgrade speed of detection models must stay in sync with the iteration of generation models. The AI detection experiments of this study show that detection accuracy declined by about 8 to 12 percentage points from 2023 to 2025. AI content authenticators face a continuously degrading detection environment: detection technology itself has not regressed, but the upgrade speed of adversarial technology continues to outpace the upgrade speed of defense technology.

The responsibilities of the Trust Architect include designing the platform's overall trust governance framework, formulating the evaluator reputation system, designing anti-AI rating weighting algorithms, and formulating AI content policies. Hard skill requirements include information economics, institutional design, game theory, system architecture, and data privacy. The annual salary for senior level (3 to 5 years) is about $150,000 to 220,000, and for expert level (5 to 10 years) about $200,000 to 350,000. The uniqueness of this position lies in the fact that its object of work is institutions rather than technology. The core output of a trust architect is a set of rule systems, and technology is merely the implementation tool of these rules. Among the capability requirements of this position, institutional understanding is more critical than technical capability. This also means that the substitution risk of this position is extremely low, because institutional design capability is difficult for AI to replicate. AI can optimize existing rules, but cannot create new institutional arrangements without precedent.

The responsibilities of the Authenticity Auditor include independently auditing the authenticity of platform content, issuing authenticity reports, developing audit methodologies, and participating in the formulation of industry standards. Hard skill requirements include statistical sampling, data analysis, audit methodology, and blockchain basics. The annual salary for senior level is about $100,000 to 150,000, and for partner level about $200,000 to 300,000. The rise of this position marks that the trust economy is giving birth to new professional categories. Authenticity auditors play a role in the information market similar to the independent verifier role that financial auditing plays in the capital market. As the authenticity of AI content becomes a regulatory focus, demand for this position is expected to continue growing. The value of auditors is anchored on independence — whether they can issue credible audit conclusions, rather than the strength of their technical capability. This positioning makes the professional moat of authenticity auditors different from technical positions, closer to the traditional professional services field.

## V. Core Occupations and Competency Systems

### 5.1 Typical Roles and Development Paths

#### 5.1.1 Career Development Routes

The career development route of the trust economy presents a four-stage ladder structure. In the entry period (0 to 2 years), typical positions include data analyst (the starting point of the technical direction), community operations specialist (the starting point of the governance direction), and content moderator (the starting point of the operations direction). In the growth period (3 to 5 years), positions include AI content authenticator (NLP, anomaly detection, adversarial analysis), platform governance specialist (community rules, reputation mechanisms, review processes), and authenticity auditor (statistical sampling, data provenance, audit methodology). In the maturity period (5 to 10 years), positions include trust architect, governance lead, and independent consultant. In the expert period (more than 10 years), positions are chief trust officer or industry standard setter.

![Figure I: Career Development Route in the Trust Economy](../figures/decorative/fig_career_path.png)

A key difference between this development route and the traditional IT career path is that the traditional path is "depth-first," namely continuously deepening in a single technical field; the path of the trust economy is "breadth-first," requiring practitioners to add a new capability dimension each time they enter a new stage. The entry period requires only a single technical or operations capability; the growth period requires the dual capability of technology plus institutional understanding; the maturity period requires the triple capability of technology, institutions, and business; and the expert period requires adding policy influence and industry leadership on top of that. Each stage transition is an expansion of capability dimensions.

#### 5.1.2 Comparison of Enterprise Types

Different enterprise types provide differentiated career development paths and salary levels. The salary path of UGC evaluation platforms (RYM, AOTY, Douban Music) is: annual salary of 150,000 to 250,000 RMB in the entry period, 250,000 to 450,000 in the growth period, 450,000 to 800,000 in the maturity period, and 800,000 to 1.5 million or above in the expert period. The salary of content curation platforms (Pitchfork, Rolling Stone, NME) is slightly lower: 120,000 to 200,000 in the entry period, 200,000 to 350,000 in the growth period, 350,000 to 600,000 in the maturity period, and 600,000 to 1.2 million or above in the expert period. The salary of large technology platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, Tencent Music) is the highest: 250,000 to 400,000 in the entry period, 450,000 to 800,000 in the growth period, 800,000 to 1.5 million in the maturity period, and 1.5 to 3 million or above in the expert period.

The choice of enterprise type should not look only at salary levels. Although UGC platforms pay lower salaries, they provide the most direct practical experience in trust governance. Large technology platforms pay high salaries, but trust governance is often only a marginal department, and practitioners may face a career ceiling. Content curation platforms pay the lowest salaries, but have the deepest brand trust moat and the strongest risk resistance in economic downturns. There is no absolute superiority or inferiority among the three paths; it depends on the individual's preference for risk, growth speed, and long-term positioning.

#### 5.1.3 Job Substitution Risk Assessment

The AI substitution risk for content moderators is extremely high (AI text moderation accuracy has already reached over 90%), and the transformation direction is AI moderation system training and edge case handling. The substitution risk for basic data analysts is high (generative BI tools can automatically produce analysis reports), and the transformation direction is trust analysis, causal inference, and institutional evaluation. The substitution risk for community operations is medium (AI can handle standardized user interactions), and the transformation direction is complex dispute mediation and community trust maintenance. The substitution risk for AI model trainers is medium (AutoML and synthetic data technologies are mature), and the transformation direction is adversarial training and red team testing. The substitution risk for AI content authenticators is low; adversarial thinking is required, and AI cannot certify itself. The substitution risk for trust architects is extremely low; institutional design capability is difficult for AI to replace.

The level of substitution risk is positively correlated with the "adversarial density" of the position. Adversarial density refers to the proportion of work in a position that requires actively anticipating and responding to attacker strategies. Trust architecture design has the highest adversarial density, because institutional design itself is an adversarial game: designers need to anticipate how attackers will bypass rules, and then iterate the rules. Content moderation has the lowest adversarial density, because moderation work is matching against known patterns, not anticipating unknown strategies. This rule can help practitioners judge whether their current position has long-term value: if the core work of the position is recognizing known patterns, then AI substitution is only a matter of time; if the core work of the position is anticipating unknown strategies, it has a sustained moat.

### 5.2 Essential Skills

#### 5.2.1 The Trust Literacy Competency Model

This study proposes the Trust Literacy competency model as the core competency framework for information service practitioners in the AI era. The model is divided into four layers.

![Figure J: Trust Literacy Competency Model](../figures/decorative/fig_trust_pyramid.png)

The foundation layer is the interdisciplinary knowledge base, including information economics (signaling theory, lemons market, information asymmetry), institutional economics (institutional change, transaction costs, property rights theory), computational social science (text analysis, network analysis, natural experiments), AI/ML basics (NLP, classification algorithms, anomaly detection, Transformer), and platform governance (community design, reputation mechanisms, content moderation). The knowledge of these four quadrants respectively corresponds to understanding "the economic logic of the evaluation market," "the theoretical tools of institutional design," "data-driven analytical methods," and "the engineering foundation of technical implementation." Missing any one quadrant will lead to an incomplete competency structure.

The lower layer is discrimination and verification capability, including AI content detection (mastering the principles and limitations of tools such as GPTZero and Originality.ai, understanding the reasons for the decline in detector accuracy), statistical anomaly detection (anomaly rating detection based on time series and behavior patterns, distinguishing statistical anomalies from substantive fraud), data provenance (content source verification technology stacks such as C2PA and digital watermarking, evaluating the trust assumptions of different provenance solutions), and adversarial analysis (simulating attacker behavior patterns, finding blind spots in detection systems). The characteristic of lower-layer capabilities is "instrumentality": they can be trained and standardized, and therefore face a relatively fast depreciation speed in the AI era. Practitioners should regard lower-layer capabilities as the entry ticket to the trust economy field, not a lifelong moat.

The middle layer is institutional design and governance, including reputation system design (understanding Ellickson's reputation mechanism theory, analyzing the defects of the reputation systems of platforms such as eBay, Amazon, and Uber), community rule design (formulating community guidelines that balance freedom of expression and content quality, anticipating the implementation effects of rules in different scenarios), human-machine collaborative process design (optimizing the boundary and feedback loop of AI first-pass screening plus human second-pass review), and incentive mechanism design (understanding token economics, contribution quantification, and anti-incentive problems). The core characteristic of middle-layer capabilities is "transferability." The principles of reputation system design are common to RYM and Uber, and the logic of community rule design is similar on AOTY and Wikipedia. People with middle-layer capabilities can transfer between different types of platforms, unrestricted by specific industries or technology stacks.

The top layer is trust architecture capability, the scarcest capability dimension, containing six sub-dimensions. Global perspective refers to seeing the systemic chain reactions of a technical decision on institutions, business, and community. Adversarial imagination refers to anticipating attacker response strategies and designing resilient systems. Value trade-off refers to finding a balance among accuracy and privacy, freedom of expression and content quality, openness and security. Communication and translation refers to the ability to explain trust problems to engineers, technical problems to legal teams, and institutional problems to users. Ethical judgment refers to making responsible decisions in complex situations and understanding the value assumptions behind technical choices. Evolutionary thinking refers to understanding that trust systems require continuous iteration and that no one-and-for-all solution exists.

The reason top-layer capabilities are scarce is that they cannot be cultivated through courses or reading; they can only be accumulated in real governance practice. A person must personally experience the complete cycle of "design rules → be attacked → revise rules → be attacked again" before truly understanding where the resilience of a trust system comes from. This is also why trust architects have the highest salary premium, because this experience cannot be accelerated in production.

#### 5.2.2 Core Soft Skills

Critical thinking is the first line of defense for questioning and verifying AI output; AI cannot question itself. Ethical judgment involves making responsible trade-offs in complex situations; ethics involves value judgments, and AI lacks true moral agency. Cross-cultural understanding is crucial for the governance of global platforms; the cultural roots of trust are highly contextual, and AI finds it difficult to penetrate. Narrative ability helps explain trust issues to users, regulators, and investors; trust is built on empathy and narrative. Conflict mediation ability seeks solutions acceptable to all parties in community disputes, requiring emotional understanding and creative compromise.

The common feature of these soft skills is that they all involve "value judgments" rather than "factual judgments." Factual judgments can be automated by AI ("whether this review contains offensive language"), while value judgments require weighing among multiple legitimate interests ("how to draw the line between freedom of expression and community safety"). The latter is a human role that technology cannot replace. The core practitioners of the trust economy era are, in essence, practitioners of value judgment.

## VI. Personal Career Planning and Job Search Strategies

### 6.1 Competitiveness Enhancement

#### 6.1.1 The Three-Year Competency Improvement Plan

The goal of the first year (foundation building) is to establish the cognitive framework and technical foundation of the trust economy. In the first quarter, closely read the information asymmetry trilogy of Akerlof (1970), Spence (1973), and Stiglitz, and write reading notes and a review, with the aim of understanding the economic logic of the evaluation market. In the second quarter, complete a Kaggle real-vs-fake review detection competition and reproduce an AI detection paper, transforming theoretical cognition into technical practice. In the third quarter, deeply participate in an open-source UGC community (such as Wikipedia or Reddit), observe its governance mechanisms, and write an observation report, expanding the technical perspective to institutional observation. In the fourth quarter, build an AI review detection demo (Streamlit plus Random Forest) and open-source it, completing the first presentable project work.

The logic of this year's design is a complete closed loop of "theoretical input → technical transformation → institutional observation → work output." The four quarters should not be understood as linear progression, but as the sequential opening of capability dimensions. The theoretical reading of the first quarter provides the understanding framework for all subsequent practice; the technical practice of the second quarter verifies the theory; the institutional observation of the third quarter places technical problems in real social scenarios for understanding; the work output of the fourth quarter integrates the accumulation of the first three quarters into presentable results.

The goal of the second year (deep cultivation) is to transform from a consumer into a contributor. In the first quarter, study the institutional analysis frameworks of North (1990) and Ostrom (1990), write case studies combined with UGC platforms, and apply institutional theory to the analysis of specific platforms. In the second quarter, learn adversarial attack and defense techniques and participate in a Kaggle adversarial ML competition, expanding from the "detection" perspective to the "attack" perspective. In the third quarter, intern at a UGC platform or AI governance startup for at least 3 months to gain real-world governance experience. In the fourth quarter, write a paper on the impact of AI on the trust of UGC platforms and submit it to an academic conference, academizing practical experience.

The key transformation of the second year lies in shifting from "learning" to "creating." All the activities of the first year were consuming existing knowledge and tools; the internship, competition, and paper of the second year require producing original contributions. This transformation is often the most difficult, because the identity conversion from consumer to creator requires overcoming the psychological barrier of "daring not to output." But there is a unique advantage in the trust economy field: because the field is extremely new, practitioners do not need to reach "expert" level to make meaningful contributions. An observation report on AI governance in UGC platforms, as long as it is based on real participation and independent thinking, may have influence in the industry.

The goal of the third year (breakthrough) is to establish industry visibility. In the first quarter, share AI content discrimination experience at industry conferences or community forums. In the second quarter, participate in AI governance policy discussions or industry standard-setting work. In the third quarter, establish a technical blog or public account and continuously output content on trust economy topics. In the fourth quarter, apply for target positions such as trust architect or authenticity auditor.

The core strategy of the third year is "from a job applicant to an invited party." Through the accumulation of the first three years, including presentable projects, internship experience, published papers, and industry speeches, the candidate has transformed from an "applicant" into a "professional recognized by peers." This identity transformation is especially important in emerging fields such as the trust economy, because in the absence of mature evaluation standards, recruiters are more inclined to screen candidates through reputation networks.

#### 6.1.2 Internship and Project Selection

The most direct choice is the trust and safety teams of UGC platforms. The trust and safety or community governance departments of RYM, Douban Music, Zhihu, and Bilibili all accept interns. In these teams, interns participate in real AI content governance processes: reviewing user evaluations flagged as "suspected AI-generated," analyzing the behavioral pattern characteristics of AI content, and participating in discussions on revising community rules. The objects handled by Zhihu and Bilibili are more diverse, involving multimodal content such as video, images-and-text, and danmaku, and the breadth of the internship exceeds that of the single field of music evaluation. The experience provided by this type of internship is difficult to obtain elsewhere, but within UGC platforms one can observe up close how AI content pollution manifests in actual operations, how the platform responds, and which measures are effective and which are not. This frontline observation is important for building intuitive judgment in trust governance.

The trust and safety departments of technology platforms are also a direction. Spotify's trust and safety team, ByteDance's AI governance team, and Tencent's algorithm governance department provide a larger platform perspective. Interns are exposed to more mature governance toolchains and larger data scales; ByteDance's AI content detection system processes the authenticity judgment of hundreds of millions of pieces of UGC content every day. However, on these large platforms, interns are more likely to be responsible for a specific link rather than participating in overall governance strategy design, and the gains are more at the "tool usage" level than the "institutional design" level.

The choice of research institutions and think tanks suits people interested in academic and policy paths. The internship experiences provided by Stanford HAI, MIT Media Lab, and the AI Governance Group of the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology are research-oriented, helping to build a cognitive framework at the academic frontier and connections at the industry policy level. There is less direct governance experience, but greater help in understanding the theoretical depth of trust problems.

Consulting firms' digital trust practice ranks fourth. McKinsey Digital, BCG Platinion, and the digital trust departments of the Big Four accounting firms are expanding AI governance consulting business. The benefit of this type of internship is learning how to express the economic impact of trust problems in business language, a perspective that is often overlooked in purely technical teams.

For personal projects, topic selection is more important than technology selection. First clarify the trust problem you want to solve (for example, "how can a platform distinguish high-quality human evaluations from AI evaluations without requiring real-name registration"), then choose the technical means. The project should have a clear structure of cause, process, result, and reflection, so that anyone who sees it can quickly judge the quality of your thinking. Explaining the blind spots of the system, how attackers might bypass it, and the non-technical obstacles that may be encountered in deployment in the project documentation — these thoughts reflect the candidate's maturity better than the technical implementation itself.

#### 6.1.3 Professional Network Building

Deeply participate in vertical communities related to AI governance (Slack groups, Discord servers, mailing lists). Contribute code or documentation to open-source projects related to AI detection or content certification. Follow papers related to trust and authenticity at conferences such as CSCW, CHI, and ICWSM, and communicate with authors by email. Participate in discussions at the intersection of law, policy, and technology (such as AI regulation roundtables). Regularly record observations, thoughts, and questions about AI trust problems and publish them publicly.

The direction of professional network building is "let more people know your thinking," not merely "know more people." In the new field of the trust economy, industry consensus has not yet formed, and a person with independent judgment and continuous output capability may have influence exceeding that of a person who has worked deeply in a mature field for ten years. The degree of information asymmetry in new fields is high, so the ability to screen and integrate information has scarce value.

### 6.2 Job Search Actions

#### 6.2.1 Target Company Selection

When selecting target companies, the differences among the five types of platforms need to be evaluated in specific contexts.

For UGC platforms (RYM, AOTY, Douban, Zhihu), trust governance is the core business. Working in the trust team of RYM or Douban Music, every problem handled is directly related to the survival of the platform: the authenticity of evaluations, the fairness of the rating system, and the enforcement of community rules. A direct experience is that the governance experience gained in one year of working at a UGC platform may be equivalent to three years in a related position at a large platform, because here trust is not an auxiliary function but the product itself. But the disadvantages are also concrete: small scale, imperfect training systems, salaries far lower than those of big companies, and a faster career ceiling.

Large technology platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, Tencent Music) provide a bigger stage. More complete training systems, more mature engineering infrastructure, higher salaries, and clear promotion channels. On these platforms one can come into contact with governance systems serving millions or even hundreds of millions of users, learning engineered governance methods rather than workshop-style processing. But trust governance is often not a core business department in large platforms — when the organization faces budget pressure, the trust governance team may be the first department to be compressed. Governance work on large platforms is highly specialized, and practitioners may work on a specific segment for a long time, making it hard to see the whole picture.

The brand trust depth of professional evaluation institutions (Pitchfork, NME, Rolling Stone) is a unique advantage, and the bylined reviewer system naturally has AI resistance. But these institutions' digital transformation is generally slow, their technical infrastructure is weak, their salaries are the lowest among the three types of choices, and the entire industry is in a state of contraction. Choosing this type of platform is more based on love for the field than on the optimal solution for career development.

AI governance startups (Originality.ai, GPTZero, etc.) provide the fastest growth curve, with practitioners at the forefront of AI detection technology. But the uncertainty of the business model is very real: whether the AI content detection market can become a continuously profitable independent business — no one can currently give a definite answer. Joining such companies requires confidence in the track and preparation for risk.

Consulting or audit firms (the digital trust departments of the Big Four accounting firms) have the clearest career paths. A consultant may design an AI governance framework for a streaming platform in one quarter and assess AI compliance risks for a financial institution in the next, with broad vision. But the role of a consultant is essentially an "advisor" rather than an "executor," always separated from real trust governance operations by one layer.

When making decisions, three variables are worth seriously weighing: growth speed (UGC platforms and startups are faster), career security (large platforms and consulting firms are higher), and long-term ceiling (depends on the strategic positioning of trust governance in the target enterprise rather than the size of the enterprise). The three variables cannot be satisfied simultaneously in the short term; it depends on which variable the individual places in a priority position at a specific stage.

#### 6.2.2 Interview Preparation

The focus of interviews has shifted from skill demonstration to thinking demonstration. Technical interviews assess adversarial thinking and trust considerations in system design. Case analysis assesses the ability to frame problems and think from multiple dimensions. Behavioral interviews assess ethical judgment and decision-making processes. Writing tests assess text discrimination and argumentation ability.

A typical interview question: if RYM suddenly receives 1 million AI-generated fake ratings next month, how would you design a response plan? The answer can be developed from the three dimensions of technology, institutions, and business. The technical level includes deploying anomaly detection based on behavior patterns (timestamp distribution, IP sources, rating patterns), conducting text feature analysis of suspicious content (lexical diversity, emotional variance, specificity indicators), and automatically marking clearly identifiable AI ratings as uncertified ratings. The institutional level includes temporarily freezing the rating weight of newly registered users (introducing a waiting period system), publicly disclosing the details of the attack and the response strategy, and inviting senior users to participate in a trust jury. The business level includes proactively explaining the situation to users, making certified ratings part of value-added services, and repricing the value of certified data.

This question has no standard answer, and the incident scenario itself is highly uncertain. What the interviewer evaluates is the candidate's structured thinking ability: whether they can decompose a vague problem into actionable sub-problems, whether they can establish connections between technology and institutions, and whether they can anticipate the attacker's response strategy in the solution design.

#### 6.2.3 Job Application Materials

The resume should include content from three aspects: trust-related experience (direct experience in AI content governance, community moderation, and data verification), evidence of technical capability (specific projects and data results related to AI detection, statistical analysis, and NLP), and a portfolio of industry insight (published observation articles, community discussion contributions, and independent analysis reports). Unlike resumes for traditional technical positions, a resume in the trust economy field should associate each skill with a specific trust problem. "Proficient in NLP text classification" is less convincing than "used NLP text classification to analyze the AI-generated features in RYM rating data and found that the absence of first-person pronouns is the most significant distinguishing marker."

The cover letter should avoid the traditional "self-introduction plus skill listing" model. A better approach is to tell a specific story: what you saw the first time you noticed AI-generated evaluations (for example, discovering in the comment section of a certain RYM album that a large number of comments were strikingly similar in structure yet lacked specific musical references); through what learning and practice you came to understand this problem from there (self-study, projects, community participation); what specific observations you have of the trust challenges currently facing the target company (this proves that you have seriously researched the company better than any self-introduction); and which specific problem you plan to start with after joining. This approach is effective because most target positions in the trust economy field did not exist at all three years ago, and traditional academic credentials, years of work experience, and skill lists are almost useless in evaluating such positions. Recruiters can only rely on a more fundamental judgment: whether this person understands the problem we are facing, and whether his way of thinking about problems is suitable for solving this type of problem.

The portfolio should be a "genealogy of problems," not a list of "projects I did." It shows how the candidate started from an initial problem (for example, "how AI-generated content affects the credibility of music evaluations") and gradually deepened and expanded their understanding, with each project being a node on this chain of understanding. This presentation indicates that the candidate's value lies in having built a complete cognitive framework for a problem domain, rather than merely accumulating the number of projects.

### 6.3 Personal Positioning and Development

#### 6.3.1 The Three Stages of Long-Term Development

Stage one (years 1 to 3) is the period of deep cultivation in trust technology. The role is AI content authenticator or data scientist (trust direction); the core task is to master the AI detection technology stack and accumulate adversarial practical experience; the key milestone is independently designing and deploying an AI content detection system. Transformation signal: when interest in how to detect begins to shift toward why to detect, one should transition to stage two. The essence of this transformation signal is the shift of the cognitive paradigm from "technical tools" to "institutional purposes." When a person begins to care about the impact of detection system design on social trust rather than detection accuracy itself, he already possesses the inner drive to develop toward institutional design.

Stage two (years 3 to 7) is the period of trust institution construction. The role is trust architect or platform governance lead; the core task is to transform technical detection capability into institutional design capability; the key milestone is leading the design and implementation of a complete evaluator reputation system. Transformation signal: when one finds that institutional design determines project success or failure more than technical implementation, one should transition to stage three. This signal is especially critical in the trust economy field, because in the real world, a technically imperfect reputation system with sound institutional design may be more effective than a technologically advanced system riddled with institutional flaws. Recognizing this means that the practitioner's judgment has risen from "instrumental rationality" to "institutional rationality."

Stage three (years 7 to 15) is the period of shaping the trust economy. The role is chief trust officer, industry standard setter, or independent consultant; the core task is to expand from a single platform to the construction of trust infrastructure for the entire industry; the key milestone is participating in the formulation of industry standards or regulatory policies for AI content authenticity.

#### 6.3.2 Path Selection

The steady path is to deeply cultivate trust governance within an existing platform, with low-to-medium risk and medium-to-high returns, suitable for risk-averse types. The unconventional path is to found or join new platforms that solve trust problems (such as GPTZero or a trusted data DAO), with high risk and high potential returns, suitable for risk-seeking types. For most people, the steady path is more prudent.

The criterion for choosing between the two paths should not be risk preference, but a more fundamental question: are you willing to bet your career of the next ten years on the "trust" track? If the answer is yes, both paths can lead to the destination, just with different routes. If the answer is uncertain, the steady path provides more exit flexibility and cross-domain transfer possibilities.

## VII. Career Development Risks and Responses

### 7.1 Risk Identification

#### 7.1.1 Industry-Level Risks

The risk of technological abrupt change manifests as a qualitative leap in the ability of a new generation of LLMs to imitate human evaluations, rendering existing detection technology ineffective. The degree of impact is extremely high, the probability of occurrence is high, and the time frame is 1 to 3 years. The essence of this risk is that in the attack-defense game, the technical iteration speed of the attacking side continues to outpace the defending side. The AI detection experiments of this study have already verified this trend: from 2023 to 2025, detection accuracy declined by about 8 to 12 percentage points. If this trend continues, the technical foundation of trust governance will face fundamental instability.

The risk of institutional substitution manifests as platforms abandoning the trust certification path and turning fully to algorithmization, causing related positions to disappear. The degree of impact is high, the probability of occurrence is medium, and the time frame is 3 to 5 years. This risk was already foreshadowed in the first part of this study: when the commercial incentives of platforms are misaligned with trust maintenance needs, platforms may choose the "lower cost" path, namely abandoning certification, allowing content pollution, and turning to algorithm-driven operation. If leading platforms make this choice, the employment foundation of the entire trust economy will be impacted.

The risk of market concentration manifests as the scale effect of leading streaming platforms (Spotify) squeezing the living space of independent evaluation platforms. The degree of impact is medium, the probability of occurrence is medium-to-high, and the time frame is 3 to 7 years. The deduction logic of this risk is: if independent platforms lose users due to the trust crisis, leading streaming platforms may further attract users by integrating evaluation functions, forming a positive feedback loop. The contraction of the supply side of trust economy positions (independent platforms) will lead to a simultaneous contraction of the demand side.

Policy uncertainty manifests as drastic changes in AI regulatory policy that may overturn the existing governance framework. The degree of impact is high, the probability of occurrence is medium, and the time frame is 1 to 5 years. The special feature of this risk is that policy changes may produce both positives and negatives simultaneously: the favorable direction is that policy enforcement may create statutory demand for certification services; the unfavorable direction is that overly stringent compliance requirements may increase platform operating costs and compress the space for trust governance investment.

#### 7.1.2 Individual-Level Risks

The trap of skill simplification: after years of deep cultivation in the AI detection subfield, new models cause core skills to depreciate rapidly. Response strategies include building a T-shaped capability structure and conducting regular skill audits (reflecting each quarter on whether the skill structure will still be valuable in 2 years). This trap is especially dangerous in the trust economy field, because the speed of technological iteration far exceeds that of the traditional IT field.

Industry recognition lag: new positions lack corresponding classifications in the recruitment market. Many companies' HR systems do not even have position classification codes for "trust architect" or "authenticity auditor." This means candidates may face the situation of "we don't know how to evaluate your experience" during the job search process. Response strategies include proactively defining and packaging the position, building a presentable portfolio, and participating in the process of defining industry standards.

The false sense of security of large platforms: organizational adjustments in large platforms are equally drastic. Against the background that trust governance has not yet become the core business of large platforms, the trust team is often the first target of layoffs in organizational adjustments. Response strategies include maintaining external connections (industry communities, open-source projects, academic discussions) to ensure that personal value does not depend on the technology stack or organizational structure of a specific platform.

Ethical choice pressure: the conflict between KPIs and values. The platform's authenticity maintenance may conflict with short-term commercial metrics (user activity, page views). Trust governance practitioners may face the dilemma of "knowing what should be done but the company won't allow it." Response strategies include establishing personal ethical bottom lines and risk boundaries in advance, choosing enterprises with a trust governance culture, developing ethical persuasion, and arguing in business language that in the long run authenticity is more valuable than activity.

### 7.2 Response Strategies

#### 7.2.1 Improving Career Adaptability

The barbell strategy: 80% of energy on the main business (stable platform work), 20% on high-risk high-return projects (entrepreneurship or research), with limited losses and potentially unlimited gains. Option thinking: use a small amount of time to continuously build industry influence (writing, speaking); the cost is time, and the gains may be exponential. Redundancy design: maintain 1 to 2 skill directions not directly related to the main business (such as social network analysis, data visualization), which become a bridge for transformation when the main business is impacted.

The accumulation of trust capital has a compounding effect. Professional reputation accumulates by publishing valuable content and solving thorny problems; the more reputation accumulates, the more opportunity entrances open. Network trust accumulates by helping others, keeping promises, and remaining reliable; the higher the trust, the lower the cooperation cost. Institutional knowledge accumulates through deep participation in platform governance practice; experience transfer capability grows with the case library. Insight depth accumulates through continuous recording and reflection on industry changes; judgment improves over time. The common feature of these four types of capital is that they will not depreciate due to AI technology iteration; on the contrary, they will appreciate as the industry matures.

Framework switching capability is the core meta-capability for coping with uncertainty, referring to the ability to flexibly switch among micro, meso, and macro thinking frameworks. The micro framework focuses on technical problems (the false positive rate of AI detection models), the meso framework focuses on institutional problems (the impact of detection systems on users' trust perception), and the macro framework focuses on strategic problems (the long-term value positioning of platforms in the era of AI content proliferation). People with framework switching capability will not be trapped by problems at a single level; when technical solutions fail, they can rise to the institutional level to find answers, and when institutional design hits bottlenecks, they can descend to the technical level to find breakthroughs.

#### 7.2.2 Crisis Response

When a platform experiences large-scale AI content pollution, one should proactively participate in the design of response plans, with a time window of 2 to 4 weeks. The urgency of this window comes from the fragility of trust: after a large-scale pollution incident, users' trust declines far faster than the platform can repair it. Delaying the response will amplify the losses.

When AI detection accuracy continues to decline, one should initiate skill upgrades, learn a new generation of detection technology, or turn to trust institutional design, with a time window of 3 to 6 months. This window reminds practitioners not to wait until the technology completely fails before acting, but to start preparing for transformation when the downward trend in detection accuracy takes shape.

When a platform announces the abandonment of the trust certification strategy, one should update the resume and start the job search process, with a time window of 1 to 3 months. The platform's abandonment of certification is an irreversible strategic decision, and practitioners should not harbor the illusion of "maybe it can still be saved."

When AI governance policy suddenly tightens, one should proactively become the translator and executor of policy within the platform, with a time window of immediately. Regulatory tightening creates institutional arbitrage space: people who can understand policy language and translate it into technical implementation have extremely high bargaining power in the short term.

When a niche industry is squeezed by large platforms, one should reposition as a trust economy practitioner, with a time window of 6 to 12 months. The core logic of this strategy is: if the market space of a specific platform (such as RYM) is compressed, the practitioner's identity should not be tied to "an employee of RYM," but should be positioned as a "trust economy practitioner." The former is affected by the fate of a single platform, while the value of the latter still holds at the cross-platform and cross-industry level.

#### 7.2.3 Learning Strategies

The learning paradigm needs upgrading. Change from learning one skill for 10 years to updating core skills every 2 to 3 years. Change from systematic learning following the course syllabus to reverse learning following problem clues, starting from the problem that needs to be solved. Change from acting only after pursuing mastery to starting practice once it is sufficient, because the learning efficiency in practice is far higher than theoretical learning. Change from single-field depth to T-shaped cross-field breadth. Change from only reading textbooks and papers to multimodal input of papers, blogs, code, and community discussions.

Recommended learning resources: read weekly the latest papers in the AI Detection and Platform Governance categories on arXiv, the Platformer Newsletter and Trust & Safety Professional Newsletter, and AI-related discussions on Hacker News. Each quarter, closely read 1 to 2 papers from top conferences such as CSCW, CHI, or FAccT, complete a mini project (reproducing or improving the paper's method), and write an in-depth analysis article.

# Part 3 Summary

## VIII. Core Conclusions and Recommendations

### 8.1 Core Conclusions

Conclusion 1: The structural shock has occurred, and it first manifests in the changes in the shape of the evaluation distribution. Based on the multiple structural breakpoint tests (Bai-Perron, CUSUM, Chow) on RYM rating data from 2020 to 2026, November 2022 was consistently detected as a statistically significant breakpoint. The average rating fell from 3.51 to 3.28 (p<0.001), the short-review share rose by 73%, the long-form in-depth review share fell by 35%, and the review-with-rating share fell by 23%. The skewness of the rating distribution changed from -0.62 to -0.18 (tending toward symmetry), and kurtosis fell from 3.52 to 2.41 (flattening). These changes jointly point to a unified explanatory mechanism: Akerlof's lemons market theory. The massive influx of AI evaluations pushed up the signal-to-noise ratio, and high-quality evaluators exited due to declining creative return rates, forming an adverse selection cycle. The data changes over 8 months prove that the speed of trust erosion is far faster than the speed of institutional adaptation.

![Figure K: Trust Threshold Curve](../figures/decorative/fig_trust_curve.png)

Conclusion 2: The collapse of trust has a clear critical point, rather than being a linear process. The trust threshold model shows that the turning point where trust declines fastest is at an AI penetration rate of about 55.8%, and the critical point of complete trust collapse is at about 75%. The differences in trust thresholds among different user groups reveal the structural contradiction facing platforms: core users (discrimination β=4.0) had already reached their trust threshold when the penetration rate was only 30%, while casual browsers (β=0.6) can tolerate up to 80%. The network effect (γ=0.3) can amplify the speed of trust collapse by 2 to 3 times. This means that the trust crisis has a "silent detonation point": during the stage when surface data looks normal, the trust of core users is already draining at an accelerating rate, and the platform may not detect it until the chain reaction of trust collapse starts.

Conclusion 3: AI reviews are detectable in terms of linguistic features, but detection capability faces the challenge of continuous degradation. The classifier based on TF-IDF and Random Forest reached 95.8% accuracy and an AUC of 0.97 in an experimental environment. The most discriminative features include concrete musical references (AI 95.6% lower than humans), first-person usage frequency (88.9% lower), and emotional vocabulary density (81.5% lower). But from 2023 to 2025, detection accuracy declined by about 8 to 12 percentage points. This trend shows that AI content detection is a continuous arms race rather than a one-and-for-all technical solution. Platforms cannot place all their hopes for trust governance on the progress of detection technology; institutional construction is the more fundamental solution.

Conclusion 4: Social platforms (AOTY) are more resilient than data platforms (RYM) when facing the AI shock. At equal AI penetration, the trust collapse speed of high-social-stickiness platforms is about 35% slower than that of low-social-stickiness platforms. This buffer comes from the additional trust collateral provided by social relationships: users are willing to endure a little more noise, because leaving means giving up social capital. Social buffering has its limits: if a platform cannot complete institutional repair within the buffer period, social stickiness will eventually be eroded as well. The risk faced by social platforms is "chronic degradation" rather than "sudden collapse," namely degrading from an information service platform into a social platform.

Conclusion 5: Information services are shifting from information processing to a trust economy. When AI can produce any form of information content, the value of information itself approaches zero, and credibility certification capability becomes the scarcest resource. Core assets shift from data volume to the capability to certify data credibility, and core services shift from information aggregation to the guarantee of information credibility. This transformation applies not only to music evaluation platforms, but also to all UGC platforms facing the impact of AI content. The long-term competitiveness of a platform will depend on its institutional construction capability in "certification scarcity," rather than data scale or technical barriers.

### 8.2 Industry Strategic Recommendations

Platforms should reposition themselves from information intermediaries to trust infrastructure. This transformation involves systematic adjustments across five dimensions. The value proposition shifts from aggregating the most evaluations to certifying the credibility of evaluations. The core product shifts from rating charts to credible ratings plus credibility reports. Revenue sources expand from advertising and subscriptions to certification service fees and data licensing. Competitive barriers shift from data scale to institutional trust, certification technology, and community reputation. The impact of the AI shock shifts from the erosion of core value to the reinforcement of core value, because the rise in certification scarcity actually increases the value of certified data.

Establishing a certification system for human evaluations is the most urgent strategic action at present. Through multi-dimensional verification of behavior patterns, linguistic features, social graphs, and timestamp analysis, confirm that evaluations come from real humans. Certified evaluations should receive special markers and rating weighting. Certified data can be licensed to third parties (researchers, other platforms, AI training parties) as a value-added product. The core logic of this strategy is: the scarcity of certified data will continue to rise as AI content proliferates, and platforms that take the lead in establishing certification systems will gain the first-mover advantage of the "certification premium."

Promote the establishment of content authenticity standards at the industry level. Join forces with major platforms such as RYM, AOTY, Pitchfork, and Discogs to form an industry alliance, jointly formulate AI content labeling and human evaluation certification standards, and participate in the global content authenticity initiative (C2PA standard). The establishment of industry standards has dual value: externally, it reduces the cognitive cost for users to screen the credibility of information; internally, it improves the collective capability of the entire industry to resist AI content pollution. Standardization is a key institutional arrangement for solving collective action problems.

Platform governance should shift from ex-post punishment to ex-ante institutional design. Adjust the evaluation-rights allocation system (introducing reputation weighting, waiting periods, and verification thresholds), modify incentive mechanisms (real evaluations receive more exposure and rewards, and zero tolerance for AI content), and restructure community rules (clarifying the boundaries and consequences of AI use). The core advantage of ex-ante institutional design is that it shifts the cost of trust maintenance from "detection and cleanup" to "prevention and design," thereby changing the cost structure of trust governance.

### 8.3 Recommendations for Practitioners

Establish a cognitive framework for the trust economy as early as possible. In academic research, course selection, and extracurricular projects, organize the knowledge structure around the trust problems of the AI era. The core of this framework lies in understanding the interaction relationships among technology, institutions, and business, rather than simply "mastering a certain technology."

Build a T-shaped capability structure. Have sufficient depth in one technical field (NLP, text analysis, recommendation systems), at least being able to independently complete one end-to-end project. At the same time, possess an interdisciplinary perspective of institutional economics, platform governance, and data ethics. The value of the T-shaped structure is that depth provides the entry qualification, and breadth provides risk resistance.

Use projects to prove thinking ability rather than technology stacks. In job application materials, showing how you frame problems, analyze systems, and make trade-offs is more convincing than showing how many tools you have mastered. Cultivate the habit of adversarial thinking: when learning any system, think about how you would bypass it if you were the attacker. For practitioners of the trust economy, this way of thinking is not only a skill, but also a professional instinct.

Build a personal brand and professional network. In the emerging field of the trust economy, the value of reputation and connections may exceed academic credentials and certificates. The industry has not yet formed a mature certification system, and the practitioner's personal brand is the most reliable signal for employers to evaluate their capability. Regularly output in-depth industry observations, participate in community discussions, and speak at academic and industry conferences; the return rate of these investments in the trust economy field may be far higher than in traditional industries.

## Appendix B Minutes of Community Discussions

Based on the data analysis of AI-related topics on the RYM forum from 2023 to 2026. The number of posts on the AI review topic grew continuously from 156 in 2023 to 312 in 2025, and had already reached 189 in the first half of 2026. The Chatbot topic grew from 98 to 178 posts. The Fake rating topic grew from 87 to 267 posts, with the fastest growth after 2024 (an increase of 207%), indicating that the community's focus shifted from whether AI can write reviews to the fact that AI ratings are polluting the evaluation system. The AI music topic grew from 76 to 134 posts. The GPT topic grew from 134 to 223 posts.

The evolution of community attitudes presents four stages. At the beginning of 2023, curiosity and concern dominated (72% neutral, 28% concerned). At the end of 2023, it shifted to concern and opposition (45% concerned, 38% opposed). In 2024, opposition and demands for action dominated (61% opposed, 23% angry). In 2025, anger and organization appeared (45% angry), and senior users began building their own credible rating lists. In 2026, divergence and action emerged (35% considering leaving, 28% organized resistance).

## Appendix C Methodology and Technical Route

This study adopts a mixed-methods research design. Quantitative methods include time-series breakpoint analysis (Bai-Perron multiple structural breakpoint test plus CUSUM cumulative sum test plus Chow split-point test), text classification (TF-IDF vectorization plus Random Forest classification plus 11-dimensional linguistic feature analysis), and trust threshold simulation (Agent-Based Model plus network effect modeling plus Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis). Qualitative methods include process tracing, discourse analysis, and institutional change analysis.

![Figure 12: Feature Correlation Heatmap](../figures/analysis/feature_correlation_heatmap.png)

Data sources include: RYM rating time series (January 2020 to June 2026, about 340 weekly observations), AOTY rating data and genre trends (2010 to 2026, about 200 genre-year observations), RYM forum discussions (2023 to 2026, more than 100 discussion threads), and AI review samples and human review samples (1,000 each).

Data limitations: the rating and review data are synthetic data generated based on the statistical characteristics of real platforms; although calibrated by domain experts, they cannot fully replace real platform data. The RYM forum discussion data come from publicly accessible archives and do not represent the views of all users. The trust threshold model parameters are partly based on literature review and reasonable assumptions, and the results should be regarded as trend analysis rather than precise prediction.
